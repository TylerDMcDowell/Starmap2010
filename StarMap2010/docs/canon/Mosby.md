Chapter 1
The early rain had burned off by mid-morning. Warm and humid, as expected for this world, the pier smelled of sea, salt, and algae carried inland by a steady breeze moving over old concrete baked smooth by decades of sun.
Caleb Mosby sat in a beach chair beneath the shadow of the battered starship that had once been an assault shuttle and now answered to Farther Star. The chair’s canvas stretched low enough to be comfortable and just inconvenient enough to discourage getting up. In one hand he held a fishing pole, the line trailing lazily into the water below. In the other was a bottle of beer that would not stay cold for long, condensation tracing slow paths down the glass. From a data pad tucked into the pocket of his sun-faded, brightly colored shirt, music drifted out in a tinny murmur. There were more bottles in the battered cooler beside him, nested in ice scavenged earlier from the Star’s galley. Fewer than there had been.
He hadn’t checked his account in a few days. He didn’t need to. It would be lower. It always was. Out here, nothing really changed unless someone forced it to.
And no one came this far unless they had a reason. No one came looking for him anymore. That had been the understanding.
The Star was his ship. A relic from the last war. Surplus, officially. But she was his. An assault shuttle once used to put men and steel into places the other side did not want them. Both the forward and aft loading ramps were down, clamshell doors open wide, letting the salt breeze roll through the cargo bay and out the far end, keeping the interior from turning into an oven beneath the tropical sun.
Brightwater was still being terraformed, nearly finished, but that had not stopped people from settling early. Shallow seas and chains of small islands drew them. Originally planned as a leisure world, it had become something else once the war shifted borders and priorities. The pier had once anchored a massive terraforming processor, long since stripped and hauled away.
The war had turned the world into a backwater. Few wanted to vacation so close to somewhere another conflict might ignite at any time. The people who came anyway called it home and, in time, developed their own unhurried way of living. They had taken Mosby in as one of their own, even if he never thought of it in quite those terms.
The song drifted to its end. A bright, professional voice followed.
“Time for news from around the galaxy. Kestralis officials remain baffled by the failure of the Veylara transit gate. Khar’Vess Dominion interference has been ruled out. Council Transit Authority investigators report no evidence yet of mechanical fault. Solarian and Astraeum negotiators have reached an impasse over the disputed system from the last war. Minister…”
Mosby reached up and tapped the pad before the next segment began. The quiet returned, water against the pilings, distant seabirds. He let it settle. He didn’t need the rest.
He rubbed the scar over his left eye, slow and absentminded. It cut pale across sun-browned skin, a clean reminder from a war that had ended before he had properly begun to age. At twenty-eight he should have looked younger. Instead, there was something settled in his gaze that belonged to men a decade further down the road.
His hair, kept short from long habit, had gone copper at the edges under the Brightwater sun. The same brown showed in his eyes. The war had pared him down to essentials, left him lean, sun-worn, built more for endurance than show.
He leaned over, pushed his hand into the cooler’s meltwater, and fished out another bottle. He didn’t look at how many were left. Droplets ran down his wrist as he turned it in his hand, thumb worrying the label until it peeled away in damp strips.
The slap of wood against metal broke the quiet, followed by the hollow clink of a paint can.
Mosby did not need to look.
But he did.
Manny was at it again.
Every so often the old man appeared without warning and began painting the hull of the Star as if it were the most natural thing in the world. Bright, unapologetic colors had long since claimed the once-drab plating. Saints with solemn eyes, icons Mosby half-recognized, symbols and fragments of scripture from faiths and stories he could not begin to name. Somewhere along the port side, grinning beneath a careful halo of gold paint, was a portrait Manny called The King.
Elvis, whoever that was.
On cargo runs, reentry heat and hard vacuum blistered the paint and burned sections away. Manny never complained. He returned with fresh cans and added more, new faces, new symbols, new blessings layered over the old.
Early on, Mosby had tried to stop it.
The old man had been halfway through a saint’s face, brush poised delicately at the curve of an eyelid, when Mosby stepped toward the hull with practical objections ready: maintenance, resale value, not wanting his ship turned into a traveling shrine.
He never spoke them.
A local boy, no more than eight or nine, skinny and sunbaked, darted in front of him and grabbed his wrist with both hands.
“No, Mister Mosby,” the boy said, eyes wide and solemn. “Bad juma if you don’t let him. Ship need blessing. You don’t stop.”
Before Mosby could answer, the boy crossed himself with exaggerated care and ran back toward town without looking back, as if the matter had already been decided by higher authority.
Mosby had stood there a moment, caught between irritation and confusion. By the time he looked up again, Manny had not paused once. The old man continued painting, unbothered, precise, as if boys issuing spiritual ultimatums were simply part of the working day.
Mosby let him finish.
Manny himself was thin and wiry, brown skin darkened and leathered by decades of salt and sun. Grey had overtaken what remained of his hair, though it clung stubbornly beneath the battered hat he wore year-round. He perched near the top rung of a ladder leaned against the hull, paint pails hanging from bent-wire hooks, brush moving as though it knew the path without instruction. The ladder swayed gently, threatening to tip with every shift of weight, yet never quite doing so.
“What are you painting this time?” Mosby called, lifting a hand to shade his eyes.
“Eyes,” Manny replied without looking down. “Ship needs eyes. Needs to see, bring you back safe.”
Mosby leaned back.
Nearly every vehicle on the islands wore color like armor. Traffic through town was an endless parade of painted machines. Bright panels, hand-drawn symbols, names in curling scripts. Smaller bikes, with no room for paint, wore tassels and streamers instead, ribbons snapping in the wind like festival banners that had forgotten the festival.
“The ship,” Manny had said once, tapping the hull with a paint-spattered knuckle, “she has a home now. And we want her know she belongs.”
“Most of that’ll burn off next reentry,” Mosby called up now.
Manny smiled at the hull rather than at Mosby. “Ship take care of town, take care of people. I take care of ship. She good ship. I don’t mind.”
A bottle later, Manny had changed sides and was nearly finished. Mosby didn’t check the cooler again. He didn’t need to. The hull looked louder now, the new eyes bright and watchful, lines sharpened, final strokes added where Manny felt they were needed.
Then the sound reached them. Engines, low and deliberate, tires grinding gravel down the road toward the pier.
Mosby noticed immediately. Most people walked the beach instead, shorter, easier, cooler under bare feet.
Vehicles meant purpose.
These meant outsiders.
Manny did not look up. He finished the line he was painting, leaned back slightly to study it.
“People come see you,” he said in the local pidgin, voice casual. “They arrive spaceport yesterday. Stay government house last night.”
Mosby didn’t like the timing. Manny never volunteered information unless it mattered.
He took another drink, frowning, his fingers drifting to the scar over his eye.
“What do they want with me?”
Manny climbed down the ladder, knees giving a quiet pop as he descended. He sealed the paint cans, wiped his hands on a rag long past redemption, and settled a battered hat onto his greying head.
“Not for me say.”
He looked at Mosby then, just once, and something in his eyes was different.
Concern.
He slung the cans into a netted carrier and started up the beach toward his house, bare feet leaving dark prints in damp sand.
Mosby watched him go.
He had no doubt Manny knew exactly who they were and why they had come.
The vehicles rolled to a stop where the road ended and gravel gave way to sand. Engines idled, then died.
The first was local police, old, worn, paint faded by sun and salt.
The others were new. Black. Clean.
Mosby had been around long enough to recognize purpose when it came looking for him.
Six people stepped out.
Two were local police, their uniforms faded by sun and salt, posture relaxed but alert. Two more wore neutrality like armor: deliberate movements, eyes always working, hands resting near concealed weapons.
The next to step out was a woman.
Dark hair pulled back with precise care. Clothing matched the security detail in tone but not in purpose, tailored, not practical. She paused, letting her eyes adjust to the light off the water.
But it was the last man who held Mosby’s attention.
Lieutenant Colonel Alastair Korrin.
No uniform. No insignia. Yet authority clung to him as naturally as breath. His complexion was a muted bronze, the color holding easily between sun and shadow. Dark hair, cut close, showed threads of silver at the temples. His features were lean and deliberate, as though shaped by discipline as much as inheritance.
His gaze swept the scene: Mosby in the chair, the beer bottle, the fishing pole. It lingered there a fraction too long. Then it reached the ship.
It stopped.
A brief, unguarded grimace crossed his face.
He took a folder from one of the security men and walked toward the pier with clipped, purposeful strides. The woman followed.
Mosby noticed she did not react to the ship’s appearance. Her eyes moved differently, cataloging hull, ramps, breeze, shoreline as if mapping the place in a single pass.
She looked human.
Something about her didn’t quite settle.
They stopped a few meters away.
Mosby shifted slightly in the chair and reeled the line in, slow and steady. The weight broke the surface without resistance, a dull sinker hanging alone on the line. No hook. No bait. There hadn’t been for days.
He caught it in his palm and let the rod rest across his lap.
“Captain Caleb Mosby,” Korrin said, voice flat.
Mosby did not stand.
“Lieutenant Colonel Alastair Korrin,” he replied just as flat. “Last I heard, you were denied promotion.”
Korrin’s jaw tightened. “We are both no longer in the Marines. I have a new position, Sector Subdirector, Solarian Intelligence.”
The pause that followed was measured. Held a fraction longer than it needed to be.
“I have an assignment for you.”
“No.”
Mosby did not raise his voice.
“I’m not in your unit. Not part of your intelligence. You don’t get to order me.”
Korrin’s eyes flicked briefly to the Star, then back.
“You misunderstand,” Korrin said. “This is not a request.”
“I’m out,” Mosby said. “That’s what discharged means.”
Color crept up Korrin’s neck.
He drew a breath.
Then the moment was gone.
The woman stepped smoothly into the space between them, altering the shape of it without challenging either man directly.
“We’re not asking you to enlist,” she said calmly. “We need you to fly. And we need you before anyone else understands why.”
Mosby said, “It’s never that simple. Why me?”
“We need someone who can go where others won’t,” Korrin said. “Someone whose loss would not require explanation. And a ship the registry has chosen not to examine too closely.”
Mosby’s gaze shifted just slightly.
The woman continued, measured.
“You have clearance histories that still resolve in the gate system. You’ve taken ships into places that don’t tolerate mistakes and returned with them intact.” 
“Without oversight. Without reinforcement,” Korrin added.
“And,” she said, “you know how to get in and out without being seen.”
Korrin folded his arms. “Don’t flatter him.”
“I’m not.”
She stepped closer.
“We are not asking you to rejoin anything. No uniform. No command structure. No flag on your hull.”
“For now,” Korrin said.
She did not contradict him.
“We are asking you to take the Farther Star somewhere we cannot officially go and return with what is found.”
“And if I decline?”
“You won’t,” Korrin said. His voice remained even. “Recall orders can still be written. They are inconvenient. They remain valid.”
Mosby studied him. “You would have to justify that.”
“I would,” Korrin said. “The decommission review on a Black Iron shuttle released to civilian registry can be reopened. Active stealth modulation grid. Hardened insertion drives. Still intact.”
He let the list stand.
“In fact, it already has.”
The wind moved along the pier and rattled a loose sheet of tin somewhere inland. No one spoke.
Mosby’s eyes flicked once toward the ship and back.
“Demobilization was chaos,” he said. “Half the fleet was processed in a month. Not every line item survived scrutiny.”
“Some line items were never meant to,” Korrin said. “And once auditors start looking, they rarely stop at one system. I could help them focus. Pending review, the vessel returns to military custody.”
Mosby did not look at the ship again.
“You try to unwind it now,” he said evenly, “and half your paperwork eats the other half. The rest starts asking how many officers signed off without reading what they signed.”
Korrin held his gaze a moment longer than courtesy required.
“You always believed you understood the system better than the people running it.”
“I did,” Mosby said. “At least once.”
The woman stepped forward again, smoothing the edge of the moment before it hardened.
“If you decline, you remain free,” she said. “There will be no arrest. No public inquiry. It will simply become harder. Licenses renew slowly. Fuel contracts complicate. Gate clearances stall.”
A brief pause.
“If you agree, nothing changes.”
Mosby allowed himself the smallest hint of a smile.
“That,” he said, “is what you call not ordering me around.”
“It’s what I call describing the world you’re already in.”
He studied her more carefully now.
“You are worse than that bastard.”
A faint twitch touched the corner of her mouth.
“May I have a moment alone with Mr. Mosby?” she asked.
She didn’t look at Korrin when she added, “You can wait by the transport.”
Korrin didn’t move.
For a moment, it looked like he might refuse.
Then his jaw tightened, and he turned away, walking back toward the vehicles with measured steps that didn’t quite hide the irritation.
He handed her the folder before he went.
When they were alone, Mosby took her in properly.
She was tall, held in a kind of deliberate balance. The humidity clung to everything on the pier except her. Not because it couldn’t, but because she refused it. Her skin caught the light differently than his own. Not paler, not darker, but layered, as though tone existed beneath tone.
Her features were precise without sharpness. When she blinked, the motion resolved a fraction slower than he expected.
Her eyes were the most striking thing about her. A deep, mineral blue that did not brighten or dull with expression. They held steady.
Not curiosity.
Assessment.
“He’s not wrong about one thing,” she said. “This situation exists whether you participate or not. We need someone who can do this quietly.”
“Who are you?”
She slid a single sheet from the folder and set it between them.
“Sael. Thalean Ministry of Security,” she said.
“I’ll be going with you.”
“That wasn’t the question.”
“It’s the part that matters.”
Mosby’s gaze flicked once to the paper, then back to her. “You don’t look like cargo.”
“I’m not.” A fractional tightening at the edge of her jaw. Gone almost before it appeared. “I’m there to ensure this remains contained.”
He stood and reached for the folder.
She didn’t hand it to him immediately.
Not long enough to challenge. Just long enough to decide.
Then she closed it.
“You know enough,” she said. “Make an informed decision.”
She handed it to him.
“I’ll be back this evening. After you’ve looked things over.”
She turned and walked back toward the vehicles. Engines started. Gravel crunched. The sound faded beyond the rise.
Mosby stood with the folder in his hand, the Star behind him, paint drying in the sun.
The tide shifted against the pilings. Somewhere inland, a generator coughed to life.
He did not open the folder.
Later, Manny returned.
He climbed the ladder and rested his hand near one of the newly painted eyes. The paint was still tacky. His lips moved, words too soft to hear.
He drew his hand back.
“They coming back,” Manny said.
“Yes.”
“You going with them?”
Mosby watched the water a long moment.
“They’ve boxed me in good.”
Manny nodded once.
“She got eyes now. That helps.”
He gathered his things and walked back toward town.
Mosby remained seated beneath the nose of the Star.
The folder rested unopened on his knee.
Above him, the newly painted eyes stared out over the water, bright and unblinking.
He took a slow drink and watched the water. It was warmer now.
He did not open the folder.
Not yet.
Once he did, it would stop being something he could ignore.
 
Chapter 2
Mosby opened the folder.
He read it once.
Then he read it again.
It was thin. Thin enough to be deliberate.
Fly to the Ael-Runn system.
Wait.
Return.
No contingencies. No timeline beyond “soon.” No explanation of what they were waiting for.
The problem wasn’t the instructions.
It was Ael-Runn.
He leaned back in the chair, the folder resting on his chest, eyes drifting to the bright line where sky met sea.
Ael-Runn sat inside the Astraeum Protectorate.
Four years ago, the Protectorate had been at war, firing on ships that flew under the same flags Mosby used to wear.
Officially neutral now. Publicly reconciled. Diplomats smiling for cameras.
But space didn’t forget as quickly as press releases did.
A patrol boat had once held Mosby’s transport group in a targeting grid for seven full minutes after the ceasefire, “confirming transponder validity.” Nobody fired. Nobody spoke. They all just waited, knowing exactly how little it would take to start things again.
That was Ael-Runn.
Most Astraeum citizens weren’t fanatics. Not loud ones, anyway. They didn’t rant. They believed. Deeply. Quietly. Structurally.
Mosby remembered an Astraeum customs officer on a border station speaking to a frightened dockworker with the same composed certainty he later used to threaten a ship’s captain with seizure. Same voice. Same calm. Same assumption that righteousness didn’t need to raise its voice. The Luminoti wasn’t decoration to them. It wasn’t something practiced on weekends. It was how law was justified. How strangers were judged. How wars were explained.
Mosby scratched at the scar over his eye.
That alone would have made the job delicate.
Doing it in a Solarian-built ship, with a Thalean operative on board, in a system that had buried people because of both of those governments...
That made it something else entirely.
He looked down at the folder again.
Wait, it said.
In Astraeum space.
Uninvited.
He closed it and considered the angles.
He could run.
Leave before they came back. Burn the registry, dump the transponder, find a place further out where no one cared who he used to be. It wouldn’t be the first time a ship disappeared between systems.
He could fight.
Make them take it in the open. Force the recall. Drag it into the light where every question they didn’t want asked would start getting asked.
Or he could take the job.
Keep the ship. Keep control. Go where they needed him to go and come back on his own terms.
Mosby looked out at the water.
Running meant living on a thin edge. Korrin wouldn’t stop. He’d have to leave Brightwater. Leave the few people who didn’t ask questions.
His eyes drifted up to the hull. Manny’s saints stared back, fresh paint catching the light.
He looked away.
Fighting wasn’t better. Korrin could bury him in paperwork and hold the Star in impound until there was nothing left to fight for.
That left one option.
Mosby exhaled slowly.
If he took the job, he kept the ship. Kept control. Set the terms as much as anyone in his position could.
And if they wanted him badly enough to come out here, they could pay for it.
His stomach reminded him he hadn’t eaten. And the sun reminded him he didn’t have all evening to pretend he wasn’t going to do it.
With a sigh, he set the folder aside and pulled out a small grill from the cargo bay. After a moment’s hesitation, he added a narrow table and two chairs.
If they were going to ask him to do something stupid, they could at least do it over decent food.
As the sun touched the ocean and the local prawns neared the end of their time on the grill, Mosby heard footsteps on the rear ramp.
He didn’t turn.
Sael came up into the cargo bay with an easy, unhurried grace, as if the ship had already agreed to her presence. She paused just inside, her attention drawn to the built-in equipment racks along the bulkhead, scarred mounts, lock points polished by use, the quiet geometry of things meant to survive hard landings.
Then she continued forward, stopping where she could see him and the water.
“I didn’t hear you drive up,” Mosby observed.
“I walked down the beach,” she said. “I don’t get to enjoy the sights often.”
“No Korrin?” Mosby asked, glancing at her.
She had changed. The field jacket was gone. In its place was a simple local dress, light fabric, muted color, cut for the heat. Her hair was loose now, falling around her shoulders instead of pulled back with military neatness.
“I thought this might go better without him,” she said.
Her gaze shifted to the grill, lingering there a moment.
“That smells good. I didn’t expect that from you.”
Mosby flipped one of the prawns, watching the shell darken and split over the coals.
“Ship rations get old after a while,” he said. “So, I learned to cook whatever the local economy has to offer. Folks in town can’t always pay in credits. I take trade. Whatever they can spare.”
“They rely on you, then.”
“Not many traders come this way,” Mosby said. “I run a regular circuit every month to Aureline, Kallamar, Parallax. Whoever still answers the relay without turning it into a negotiation. I was due to bring back microbiome stabilizers and some other supplies for the hospital on this run.”
He transferred the prawns to a plate. Steam curled into the cooling evening air.
“It doesn’t make much more than fuel and maintenance,” he added, “but I don’t need much more. Keeps things from breaking. Medicine. Mail. The small stuff that turns life or death when it runs out.”
Sael watched him for a moment. Not his face, but his hands, the careful economy of his movements, the way the ship stood open behind him like part of the conversation.
“Mind if I sit?”
“Go ahead.”
She took the chair across from him. As he set the plate down and reached for the wine, Mosby caught the faintest trace of something unfamiliar when she shifted in the breeze.
Not perfume. Spice. Not local. Too clean to be accidental.
He didn’t comment on it.
But he noticed.
“You’ve had time to read the dossier,” she said as he poured the wine and handed her a glass.
“I did.”
Mosby sat and served a portion onto each plate. The prawns steamed faintly between them. He didn’t rush to eat.
“There wasn’t much to it,” he said. “We leave here, head to Ael-Runn, land, wait while you do whatever it is you’re there to do, then come back.” He glanced at her over the rim of his glass. “If that’s really all there is, you could’ve booked passage on a liner. Cheaper. Quieter.”
Sael took a sip of wine, held it a moment, then set the glass down. Her movements were unhurried. Precise.
“You know it isn’t that simple.”
Mosby let out a short, humorless breath. “Yeah. I do.” He leaned back slightly. “And it’s going to be rougher than you’re letting on.”
She didn’t interrupt.
“Ael-Runn sits inside the Protectorate,” he continued. “They’re touchy about who comes into their space on a good day. After the war?” He shook his head. “They don’t see outsiders as visitors. They see them as problems.”
He picked up his fork, more out of habit than appetite.
“And that’s before the Luminoti.”
Sael’s expression didn’t change, but something behind her eyes did.
“Of course,” she said. “That’s why you’re flying me in.”
She took a bite, then paused. Her expression shifted, not surprise, but recognition.
“This is good.”
She took another bite before continuing, as if the food had earned her patience.
“You flew into worse places during the war. I read your file. Even the classified parts.” Her gaze held his. Steady. Present. “At least no one is shooting at you this time.”
Mosby set his fork down.
The sound was soft. Deliberate.
“We won’t have a team backing us up either,” he said. “No overwatch. No extraction window. Just you, me, and the Star.”
Sael didn’t argue.
“I can fly her,” Mosby continued. “Keep us out of trouble most days. But when this goes sideways, and it will, I’m going to need someone who can keep the ship working while I’m busy doing something else.”
He watched her carefully.
“Engineering. And it wouldn’t hurt if they knew how to move in places where questions come with consequences.”
Sael took another bite, slower this time.
“You already have someone in mind.”
Mosby took a sip of wine, then nodded once. “An old contact.”
She didn’t ask more.
“Two hundred thousand credits,” Mosby said after a moment. “Half up front. Another hundred to cover the engineer.”
He didn’t look away.
Sael didn’t blink.
She set her glass down and extended her hand across the table.
“Agreed.”
The word came too quickly.
Mosby didn’t take her hand.
“You didn’t even ask why.”
“I don’t need to,” she said. “That number tells me how dangerous you think this is. And how much you expect the truth to cost once it finally shows up.”
A faint breath left him. Not quite a laugh.
“You’re not even pretending this is a negotiation.”
She met his eyes. Calm. Certain.
“You weren’t pretending either.”
For a moment they sat there, the sound of the tide moving beneath the pier filling the space between them.
Then Mosby took her hand.
Her grip was firm. Not dominant. Not tentative. Just sure.
“How long before we have to leave?”
“As soon as you can be ready,” she said. “I wouldn’t take longer than a few days.”
The evening settled around them, warm and salt-heavy, while the ship loomed quiet and watchful behind her, eyes freshly painted, staring out toward a future neither of them fully trusted.
The next two days started early.
When the Relay office opened, Mosby was already waiting outside with a cup of bitter station coffee and a list of names he hadn’t used in a while. The Relay handled off-world messages, sending compressed bursts through a micro-gate link to the wider network. He sent notes to traders he knew and trusted, people who understood quiet work and didn’t ask more questions than necessary. Replies would take time. He was buying it in every direction he could.
After that, he arranged for cargo to be ready for loading in two days’ time, then returned to the Star and began moving through her methodically, securing anything loose that might rattle free under hard thrust.
The afternoon and the next day were spent checking every system, especially the forward flanks where Black Irons took stress on violent entries.
He worked with a light clamped between his teeth more often than not, collecting minor cuts on each hand and grease up to his elbows as he pulled open maintenance hatches that hadn’t been touched in months.
The ship smelled different when she was opened up like this. Metal, insulation, the faint sharp tang of coolant.
Sael appeared at some point without announcement.
He found her in the forward systems bay, sleeves rolled, diagnostic slate open, recalibrating a sensor cluster that had been drifting off true for longer than he liked to admit.
“You don’t have to do that,” he said, leaning against the hatch frame.
“It was wrong,” she replied without looking up. “If I’m risking my life in this ship, I want it right.”
There was no accusation in it. Just fact.
He watched a moment longer, then nodded once and moved on.
The next morning, when the haulers arrived with the cargo, she took charge of the loading without asking permission.
Most of it was seafood packed in insulated crates, with a smaller amount of local produce bound for markets farther along Mosby’s usual route. The beach road filled with the smell of brine and engine exhaust as the haulers idled with their ramps down.
Sael moved through the cargo bay with easy competence. She balanced loads across the deck without being told how, keeping heavier crates low and forward and leaving the centerline clear. Nothing went near the access hatches. Nothing where it might shift under lateral thrust.
When she noticed Mosby watching, she said simply, “I worked logistics. Started as a loader. Moved up from there before getting into government work.”
Mosby shrugged. That tracked. He let her continue.
By noon, most of the cargo had been secured, netting cinched tight, tie-downs checked twice. The last hauler rolled away down the beach road, leaving tire tracks that would be gone by morning tide.
Manny came walking along the sand as if this were any other day.
Trousers rolled. Hat in hand. The slow, patient stride of a man who had never hurried for someone else’s schedule.
He carried a small envelope pouch tucked under his arm. Inside were printed relay texts, the replies to the messages Mosby had sent the morning before. Short, practical ones. A woman on Aureline who owed him a favor. A retired courier on Kallamar who still liked to feel useful. Each had agreed to cover the route Mosby normally ran until he returned.
After reading them, Mosby handed the messages back to Manny.
“Things’ll be fine till I get back,” he said. “Jacobson might overcharge a bit, but I can cover that later.”
“Town good to know,” Manny said, eyes moving over the ship, taking in the change in her usual relaxed, open feel. The ramps were still down, but the air around her had shifted. Less harbor. More departure.
Then Manny brought out what he’d been carrying in his other hand.
A necklace of bright braided cord, strung with small shells and a carved turtle of dark driftwood, worn smooth from handling.
Without ceremony, Manny slipped it over Mosby’s head and let it settle against his chest.
“What’s this for?” Mosby asked, lifting the turtle between thumb and forefinger.
Manny shrugged. “For the journey. Turtle knows. Comes back to same shore.” He nodded once toward the ship. “Figure you should too.”
He didn’t wait for thanks. He simply turned and started back toward town.
“Hey,” Mosby called after him, “how do you know about turtles? Terraformers haven’t brought any in yet.”
Manny raised a hand without looking back.
“Don’t need to see them,” he called. “I know.”
Sael was tightening the last cargo strap when Mosby came up the aft ramp, boots ringing softly on the metal decking.
“We good to go?” he asked.
“Last one,” she said, giving the strap a final pull. “Doesn’t shift under lateral thrust now.”
“Good.” He glanced toward the stack of cases near the bulkhead. “Got all your gear?”
She pointed. “Everything I need.”
“Fine. You’ve got the copilot’s bunk. Stow your gear there. Any firearms go in the weapons locker,” Mosby said, reaching past her to thumb the control panel. The aft ramp began to rise, hydraulics whining as the clamshell doors folded inward and sealed with a heavy thud.
“Why is that?” she asked.
“My ship, my rules.” He gave her a level look. “I don’t want anything loose that could be trouble once we’re underway. You don’t like it, we can call this off right now.”
Sael held his gaze a moment longer than necessary. Then she nodded once. “Fine. But I want the access code.”
“I figured as much,” Mosby said, already turning away toward the forward passage.
Twenty minutes later, Mosby sat in the cockpit, running through preflight. The boards glowed in layered colors, status bars creeping steadily toward green as systems came online. He moved through the checklist by habit, fingers flipping guarded switches, eyes skimming readouts that had become as familiar as his own hands.
Sael entered quietly and slid into the copilot’s seat, fastening her harness with efficient, practiced motions.
“Are we ready?” she asked.
“Just about,” Mosby said. He keyed the transmitter.
“Brightwater Port Control, trader vessel Farther Star, requesting flight plan activation.”
A moment passed. Then an out-of-breath voice came back, slightly distorted by the old relay equipment. “Farther Star, Brightwater Control, go ahead.”
Mosby smiled faintly and continued working. The ship’s systems deepened in tone as the reactors shifted from standby to flight readiness. A low vibration built through the deck, not loud, but full of restrained promise, like a big animal thinking about running.
“Filed outbound to Aureline via direct burn,” he said. “Two souls aboard, cargo secured. Request plan activation and departure clearance.”
Sael raised an eyebrow. “Being a bit officious, aren’t you?”
“They don’t get much traffic,” Mosby said. “Makes them feel important. Keeps me in practice too.”
The voice came back. “Plan activated at 13:12 local. No other departures scheduled. You’re clear whenever ready, Mosby.”
“Brightwater Traffic, Farther Star lifting in two minutes.”
“Copy that. Surface corridor clear. Winds light out of the west. You mind if my cousin uses your pier while you’re gone?”
Mosby chuckled. “Sure. Tell him just don’t scuff anything.”
“No promises,” the voice replied.
Mosby eased the throttles forward.
The Farther Star lifted fast and clean, rising off the pier in a column of shimmering heat. The water below flattened under the thrust wash, ripples racing outward from the pilings as the ship climbed. The pier, the town, and the scatter of painted rooftops dropped away beneath them.
Blue faded to indigo, then violet. The horizon curved, subtle at first, then undeniable. Clouds thinned below like torn gauze, their tops catching sunlight that no longer reached the ground.
The vibration shifted as the atmosphere pressed against the hull, then began to fall away. A shudder ran through the frame as they punched through a band of rough air.
Sael’s fingers tightened on the armrests.
“You all right?” Mosby asked, not looking away from the instruments.
She nodded once, too quickly. “Not used to this much turbulence.”
Mosby’s mouth twitched. “Lady, you ain’t seen nothing yet.”
Her head snapped toward him, eyes widening just a fraction.
His grin deepened, but his hands never stopped moving.
“Brightwater Traffic, Farther Star passing six-zero thousand meters, transitioning to orbital climb,” he transmitted.
A moment later the reply crackled through. “Logged, Farther Star. Safe flight, Mosby. Relay office will forward any messages.”
The sky darkened toward black ahead of them, and the first hard stars began to burn through.
Behind them, Brightwater turned in the light.
Ahead, Ael-Runn was waiting.
Mosby adjusted the throttles by a fraction, more out of habit than need.
He preferred jobs where he knew what he was flying into.
This wasn’t one of them.

 
Chapter 3 
Clearing the planet’s gravity well took longer than most people expected, and Mosby wasn’t pushing the engines. Their power came as a steady vibration through the ship—a low tremor in the deck and up through the seat frame. The climb was deliberate, efficient, unhurried, stingy with fuel as the Farther Star curved away and Brightwater fell behind.
Sael said little during ascent. Her hands stayed on the armrests, though the death grip she’d started with had eased. As violet faded to black and the world slipped out of view, she settled back and took several slow breaths.
“You, okay?” Mosby asked, adjusting course, his hands moving easily across the controls.
“I…” She grimaced. “That felt rougher than I’m used to.”
“You’re used to civilian craft. They run heavier stabilizers and dampeners than what the Star was built with,” he said. “I should apologize. Assault pilots turn the dampeners down to get a better feel for the ship going in on a run. Rides rougher, but that’s what I’m used to.” He glanced over, sincere. “I’ll reset them all the way up next time.”
Sael closed her eyes and let out a slow breath. “No… I’ll manage. You do what you need to get us there.”
Two hours later Mosby began the FTL checklist, eyes moving across displays and instruments as his hands worked the controls by habit. Sael had relaxed once they were clear of the planet. She returned from the galley with two mugs and offered one across the console.
“Coffee?”
“Oh, no thanks,” Mosby said. “Four and a half years in the Marines and I never could get used to the nasty stuff.”
Sael settled into the copilot’s seat, cupping the mug in both hands. “It doesn’t grow on Astrin, and we couldn’t afford it anyway.” She took a sip, closing her eyes briefly as she savored it. “I learned to like it working logistics on a station in Orenai.”
“Orenai?” Mosby glanced at her. “That’s Solarian.”
Sael lowered the mug to her lap, still holding it. A faint crease formed between her brows. “I’m the fourth daughter of a minor house. There weren’t many prospects for me, so I took a government post. They assigned me to Orenai as a logistics liaison.”
“So, you were a Lady stacking boxes,” Mosby observed, continuing down the checklist.
“Yes,” she said, straightening in her seat, chin lifting a fraction. “But it got me to where I am now—and where I will be soon.”
Mosby paused at that, one hand resting lightly on the console. Then he said, as if nothing of weight had just been spoken, “Well, do you mind checking the cargo before we go to FTL?”
Sael studied him for a moment, then her expression softened. “Certainly.” She set the mug into its holder, unstrapped, and moved aft.
Mosby watched her go, gave a small shake of his head, and turned back to the instruments.
“All good?” he asked when she returned.
“Everything secure,” she said, settling into her seat. “But the ship’s going to smell like fish soon.”
He sighed. “Can’t be helped. Not much else comes off Brightwater until the harvest comes in. I’ll get new filters at Aureline Transit, but it’ll linger for a month.” He tapped the final confirmation. “All right—let’s go to FTL.”
Sael tightened her straps.
“Don’t overdo it,” Mosby said. “This won’t be like leaving atmo. This’ll be… different. Smooth, but different.”
She looked relieved. “I’ve been through jumps before, but always in liners or cargo haulers. I’m a rated pilot, you know. Those ships flew almost entirely by instruments.” She gestured toward the forward ports. “You couldn’t see a thing outside.”
Mosby leaned back slightly. “Okay… here we go.”
The FTL drive began to spool up, and the ship filled with a low, steady hum.
The world outside changed.
FTL wasn’t a flash or a streak of stars. Instead, space ahead softened, like heat haze over a desert road. A faint shimmer gathered before the bow, then spread, blooming outward until it swallowed the view and even the Farther Star seemed dim beside it.
Outside, the universe stretched.
Stars did not streak—they bent, smeared into impossible geometries, long curved distortions like light seen through rippling water. Shapes lingered at the edge of perception: threads, arcs, deep wells of shadow that seemed to slide past without ever quite moving.
Inside, a low harmonic vibration filled the ship—not loud, but present everywhere at once. Now and then came the subtle sensation of turning, though no thrusters fired and no course change showed on the board.
Sael exhaled slowly. “It looks different than I expected. It feels like we’re inside something.”
“We are,” Mosby replied. “Just don’t ask me to explain what.”
After that there was little to do once the navigation computer took over. Mosby stayed near the cockpit, dozing in short stretches, waking often to scan the instruments or when a subtle shift in the ship’s motion stirred him.
When the second watch cycle would normally begin, Sael appeared with food and two cups.
“You sleep like a man expecting problems,” she said, setting the tray down.
Mosby’s eyes opened partway. “I sleep like a man who’s had problems,” he said. “Another old habit from flying alone so long. There isn’t anyone else.” He pushed himself upright and began eating. “Pretty good,” he added.
“Thanks.” She checked the instruments. “It isn’t good for you. You should get proper sleep. I can handle things, and you won’t be any use worn out if something happens.”
“Alright,” Mosby said, standing and stretching. “Let’s see what you can do.”
Sael slid into the seat. He stood behind her, watching—smooth inputs, no overcorrection, her eyes traveling steadily across the board.
“What do you think?” he asked.
“Transit’s faster than a hauler, but nothing I can’t handle. Not much to do, really.”
She waited until he’d gone. “I know,” she said softly, with a small smile he didn’t see.
The next day passed in the quiet rhythm of ship life. One of them always near the cockpit. Meals, watches, checks—the same small loop, over and over. As Sael had said, there wasn’t much to do.
On the third day, a chime sounded.
“FTL exit approach in twenty,” Sael said over the intercom.
“I’ll be there in a minute,” came Mosby’s reply.
He entered the cockpit, stretching. “How are things?”
“Everything within parameters,” she said, starting to rise.
Mosby put a hand on her shoulder. “You ever piloted an exit?”
“Once. It was a while back. Mostly automated.”
“Well, this is a quiet system. No better time to do it manually.” He slid into the copilot’s seat.
Sael took a breath and began the transition. Slowly, space outside clarified. The stretched impressions of stars tightened back into points. A pale system primary bloomed ahead.
“Easy,” Mosby murmured. “Don’t rush the transition.”
“I’m not. Sensors show no obstructions at exit point.”
The field peeled away like water shedding from a hull. Normal space settled around them with a subtle shudder.
Sael let out a breath.
“Welcome back to the slow universe,” Mosby said.
Traffic lanes resolved. Civilian transponders pinged lazily—haulers, couriers, a bulk transport crawling in-system.
Routine. Orderly.
Mosby keyed the comm. “Aureline Traffic, independent trader Farther Star inbound from outer vector. Request approach corridor and docking assignment.”
“Farther Star, wait one at the outer marker.”
“Mosby,” Sael said, a note of concern in her voice, “transient return on the scope.”
“Where?” he said, leaning to get a better look.
She marked the vector, but it now showed empty space. “Sensor echo from the transition?” she asked, not sounding certain.
Mosby didn’t answer.
A second later the contact returned—closer this time, still outside visual range, profile too thin for a freighter, too steady for debris.
And matching their vector.
Mosby’s jaw tightened. “That’s not traffic.”
The contact shifted again, sliding just far enough off the sensor edge to make a clean lock impossible.
Sael’s voice stayed level, but her hands were very still on the controls. “It’s pacing us.”
“Yeah,” Mosby said quietly. “It is.”
He checked the sensors and the comm. No registry string. No trade ID. Just a clean, blank return where data ought to have been.
He stiffened.
Sael noticed the change in his posture. “Problem?”
“Maybe nothing,” he said. “Got a ghost out there.”
He magnified the contact. The return was thin, almost too neat—not quite a hull so much as a void cut from space where a hull ought to be.
“Military?” she asked.
“Not broadcasting like one,” Mosby said. “Not broadcasting like anything.”
He keyed a polite, low-power ping on standard trader frequencies. “Unidentified traffic, this is independent trader Farther Star inbound from Brightwater. You showing passive only, or you got something to say?”
No response.
The contact held its course for several minutes more, then made a shallow burn adjustment. Not toward them. Not away. Just… sideways, as if stepping out of a line only it could see.
A minute later it disappeared from the scanner display, gone as cleanly as it had appeared.
Sael kept her eyes on the screen even after the marker vanished. “Could be a survey craft,” she said. “Or a courier running dark.”
“Could be,” Mosby agreed.
He didn’t add what he was thinking. Survey craft filed notices. Couriers running dark did not drift that close to a known trade lane without at least a token hello.
“What do you think?” Sael asked, more quietly now.
“Don’t know,” Mosby said. “I’m just a pilot. This is more your world than mine.”
She watched him for a moment, calculating, lips pressed tight, then looked back at the scanner. “It’s probably nothing to worry about.”
Mosby still didn’t answer. He only eased his hand back onto the controls, as if a steadier grip could make the universe behave.
Then the comm came to life. “Farther Star, Aureline Control. You’re cleared Dock Ring C, Berth Twelve. Vector two-seven-eight mark zero-zero-three. Watch your drift—we’ve got a tanker cycling thrusters on the inner lane.”
“Copy that,” Mosby said, switching seats with Sael.
He made the course corrections smoothly and guided the Star through the traffic lanes. Small adjustments brought them into alignment with the berth. A soft shudder ran through the hull as the docking clamps took hold. Mosby powered down the engines and let the station’s handling system ease the Star the rest of the way in.
Sael watched as he completed the docking sequence. “You make that look easy.”
“It is,” he said. “Right up until it isn’t.”
The smell reached the dockworkers before the ramp had finished lowering.
One of them wrinkled his nose, then grinned. “Brightwater catch again?”
“Fresh this time,” Mosby called down.
The air in the cargo bay was still cool from transit, but the sharp brine of packed fish cut through insulation and cold metal alike. The workers came aboard with practiced efficiency, boots ringing on the deck plates as they rolled in pallet jacks and shoulder braces. Crates of insulated seafood went first; their lids beaded with condensation. Then came the smaller bins — leafy island greens wrapped in damp cloth, knobby root vegetables still carrying a trace of red soil in their creases.
Payment transferred in small, practical numbers. No ceremony. No bargaining theater. Just figures reviewed on a handheld slate, a nod, and a confirmation tap. Not enough to make anyone rich, but enough to keep routes alive and names remembered.
Sael handled the manifests without being asked. She stood just off the main flow of traffic, slate in hand, cross-checking weights against declared loads and verifying signatures with quick, precise motions. Her expression stayed neutral, but she worked with the quiet intensity of someone who did not like leaving loose ends behind.
Across the bay, Mosby leaned against a bulkhead, speaking with the dock receiver while two workers pried open the lid of a seafood crate.
A breath of cold brine rolled out as the seal cracked.
The receiver prodded one of the inner packs with a gloved finger, then frowned. “Ice is softer than it should be.”
“It always is by the time it gets here,” Mosby said. “Your loading bay sat open half the transfer window.”
The man pressed a thumb against a gel pack, watching it deform. “Might have to mark this as partial temperature compromise.”
“Might,” Mosby agreed. “If you want the paperwork headache. Or you can call it within tolerance and move it while it’s still good.”
The receiver glanced at him sideways. He knew the dance as well as Mosby did. Mark it spoiled and the forms multiplied. Mark it usable and everyone kept their day simple.
“How far over do you think you are?” the man asked.
“Less than two percent,” Mosby said. “Same as every other time your dock runs behind schedule.”
A pause.
The receiver grunted. “Call it acceptable loss.”
“Thought you might.”
By the time the last Brightwater crate rolled down the ramp, the cargo bay was already filling again. Machine parts in reinforced cases. Medical supplies with tamper seals and temperature tags blinking steady green. A row of sealed containers marked for Karamar and further along the line, their destination codes stenciled in faded blue.
Routine. Ordinary. Safe.
Which made Mosby more uneasy than he liked.
He stood at the top of the ramp a moment longer than necessary, watching the dock beyond the workers’ backs. Cranes moved. Cargo floated between guide beams. Station traffic drifted in its slow, deliberate patterns.
Nothing there.
He rubbed the scar over his eye with his thumb. The shadow contact lingered in his mind, wrong in a way he couldn’t name.
He had seen a shape once, out in open space during the war. Black against black, a hull line that didn’t reflect starlight the way it should have. Just a presence where no presence ought to be, sliding across his sensors and then gone.
He filed a report.
The report disappeared.
Nothing he could point to. Nothing he could prove.
That was what he didn’t like.
Behind him, paperwork finished cycling through the dock net, confirmations chiming softly from the cargo office console. Sael stepped up beside him.
“Want to get something to eat that isn’t ship rations?” Mosby asked.
“Know any place that’s good?”
“Maybe. I want to check for messages on the way.”
Inside the station, gravity felt wrong after three days under steady thrust.
The deck didn’t move, didn’t hum with the faint, reassuring vibration of engines working. It just sat there, solid and indifferent. Mosby’s stride shortened without his permission, knees flexing a little more than needed as if the floor might shift. He didn’t bother trying to hide it.
Sael adjusted within a dozen steps. Her balance settled, shoulders squaring as though she had changed environments by choice rather than necessity.
They stopped first at the relay office, a narrow room tucked between a supply depot and a fuel broker. The air smelled faintly of hot circuitry and old paper.
The clerk behind the counter barely looked up. “Name?”
“Mosby. Trader.”
She tapped a key, reached into a tray without turning her head, and slid a thin stack of printed slips across the counter. “Three inbound. Two pending transmission confirmation.”
Mosby picked them up and stepped aside. The slips were thin, cheap paper — something about physical copies still made people feel better when signals went bad.
Short notes. Route confirmations. A polite complaint about a pickup delay he would have to smooth over later.
If later came, he reminded himself.
Nothing else.
Sael watched him read, her hands folded loosely behind her back. “You were expecting something?”
“Not really,” he said, folding the slips once and tucking them into his jacket. “Just checking who’s still talking.”
They ate in a dockside restaurant that specialized in food meant to remind travelers of somewhere else.
The lighting was warm and low, the walls decorated with faded prints of coastlines and farms from half a dozen worlds. The air carried a blend of spices that didn’t quite belong together but tried very hard to pretend they did.
Sael tried a local stew and decided, after two careful bites, that it was attempting three cuisines at once and succeeding at none of them. She ate it anyway, methodically, as though it were a task that required completion.
Mosby stuck with bread, roasted meat, and something that claimed to be beer. He drank it without comment, which was comment enough.
They sat near a wide panoramic viewport overlooking one of the outer docking arms. Traffic drifted past in slow, deliberate motion: tugs nudging cargo modules, a shuttle rotating on thrusters before easing toward a berth, running lights sliding across the glass like distant stars that had forgotten how to stay still.
Mosby’s gaze kept straying back to the port.
Not staring. Just… checking.
Sael noticed on the third time. “You’re expecting someone to wave?”
“Wouldn’t mind a friendly face,” he said.
His eyes shifted again, tracking a vessel easing out from behind a lattice of gantries. For a moment all he saw was reflected glare — then a dark hull line, angular, unadorned, slipping sideways under maneuvering jets.
It might have been the same ship.
Might not.
By the time he leaned forward slightly, trying to catch a registry mark, a fuel tender drifted between it and the viewport. When the view cleared, the ship was already turning away, vanishing behind the curve of the station’s outer ring.
He sat back.
Sael set her spoon down quietly. “Problem?”
“Next stop is Karamar,” he said, as if she had asked something else. “Then New Horizon. Traffic will be heavier there. Good place to disappear in a crowd.”
“Is that possible in the Star, the way she’s painted?” she asked lightly.
He gave her a sour look over the rim of his mug.
She held his gaze a moment longer, the faintest hint of a smile at the corner of her mouth. “You think we’re being followed.”
“I think something took an interest,” he said. “That’s not the same thing. Yet.”
She didn’t push further. But when she lifted her spoon again, she didn’t taste the stew. She was looking past her reflection in the viewport, into the slow dance of ships beyond the glass. Her eyes weren’t searching space. They were measuring it.


 
Chapter 4
Departure from Aureline was clean. Gate traffic was steady but tightly controlled, ships spaced at precise intervals, each holding its assigned vector like freight cars in an invisible train.
Sael leaned forward slightly as the gate complex grew in the forward ports.
It was not a simple structure.
Two immense rectangular loops hung in space, joined at the middle by a thick structural spine. Trusses, emitter rails, and armored conduits laced the framework, giving the whole construct the look of industrial machinery built on a scale that dwarfed warships. One loop handled departures, the other arrivals, their fields kept separate by dense hardware at the narrow waist between them.
Faint distortions shimmered along sections of the framework, like heat haze over metal, where containment fields pressed against the vacuum. Thin bands of polarized light slid along emitter tracks as the system constantly recalibrated.
Ships ahead approached the outbound lobe one at a time. Each reached an invisible boundary, wavered in a soft gravitational shimmer—
and vanished.
“First time through a major gate from the front seat?” Mosby asked, eyes on the approach display.
“I’ve used them,” Sael said. “Never from up here.”
“Then don’t blink.”
The flight computer chimed as it accepted the gate’s approach envelope. Thrusters whispered in short, precise bursts, trimming their vector by fractions too small for human hands to manage cleanly.
A calm voice came over the comm.
“Outbound vessel Farther Star, confirm mass profile one-one-eight thousand metric and lane assignment Delta-Seven.”
Mosby thumbed the transmit. “Farther Star confirms. Delta-Seven.”
“Lane lock verified. Maintain envelope. You are cleared to threshold on mark.”
The console tone shifted, subtle but final. Lane confirmation pulsed green. The ship was no longer navigating toward the gate. It was being guided into alignment.
“Crossing threshold,” Mosby said.
The forward view did not flare or flash.
The stars thinned, smeared—
and were gone.
For an instant there was a hollow sensation, not in her body but in her sense of distance, as if the universe had stepped back.
Then the tunnel resolved around them.
They were inside a corridor of structured spacetime. Layered geometries flowed past in vast, slow patterns. Planes of shifting color. Lattices folding through one another. Curves that seemed to rotate without turning. Nothing repeated. Nothing held still long enough to be named.
Sael’s fingers tightened on the armrests before she noticed. “I thought it would be more dramatic.”
“Be glad it isn’t,” Mosby said. “Dramatic means something’s wrong.”
Gate transit felt nothing like direct FTL. There was no engine push, no sense of acceleration. It was as if the ship had been caught in a current far larger than itself, carried along a channel carved through space. The controls showed no meaningful authority. They were passengers.
Ahead, faint and distant, another ship rode the same lane. Its navigation lights held at a constant separation, neither growing nor shrinking.
To their right, something flared.
A streak of blue-white light tore past in a neighboring transit stream, Doppler-shifted into a hard ultraviolet glare that left a brief afterimage before it vanished into the flowing geometry.
She blinked. “What was that?”
“Another lane,” Mosby said. “Different mass class, different transit rate. Gates run a lot of streams side by side. We only ever see the edges.”
The Star hummed steadily, but the sound came from its own systems rather than propulsion. Field stabilizers murmured. Air fans whispered. Inside the hull, everything was ordinary.
Outside, reality had been turned into infrastructure.
“This time,” Mosby added, “there’s even less to do than in FTL.”
The navigation computer did not steer. It confirmed they remained centered in their assigned flow. Deviation wasn’t permitted. Maneuver wasn’t possible. Even so, Mosby’s eyes lingered on the sensor display longer than they needed to. No contacts. No variance.
He kept looking a moment longer, then forced himself away.
Transit time to the far gate: fifteen days.
Long enough for routine to settle in. Filters changed. Diagnostics run. Small work to measure time.
On the fourteenth day of transit, they sat together in the cockpit, watching the slow drift of structured light beyond the forward ports. It was like staring into a river made of mathematics.
Sael let the quiet stretch.
“What is it between you and Korrin?” she asked. “Why do you hate each other so much?”
Mosby didn’t answer right away. His eyes stayed on the instruments.
“I started out as just another assault pilot for the Marines,” he said at last. “We were on one of those planets I can’t even remember the name of anymore, flying evac. One of our battalions got cut off in a mountain valley. The Wards had the area thick with anti-air, and our people were getting picked off trying to move the wounded.”
Sael’s brow furrowed slightly. “Wards?”
“What we called them,” Mosby said. “We were Marines, Soldiers, Sailors. They called their troops Stewards.”
A small pause.
“It irritated them when we shortened it.”
The shifting light from the corridor slid across the canopy and over his face.
She noticed how still he had become.
“I went in low and as fast as I could push the shuttle,” he went on. “Took fire on the way down, but I got the first load out. Shuttle came down hard on the second run. I swapped birds and kept going.”
He shifted slightly.
“I made three more runs. Lost two more shuttles. Took shrapnel on the last one and didn’t know it until we were clear.”
“Sounds bad,” she said.
Mosby nodded once. “We did what we had to. Most didn’t make it out.”
The corridor flowed on outside.
“When I got out of the hospital, I’d been transferred. Korrin was CO of a special operations unit. Wanted me because I’d been reckless enough and good enough for the kind of missions he was handing out.”
A faint twitch at the corner of his mouth.
“I didn’t like being pulled off evac for covert ops that didn’t make sense at the time. Though I did get to fly the Star.”
Sael turned toward him. “You flew this ship in the war?”
“Sure did. She was something then.” The hint of a smile faded. “Toward the end, we were on a planet, Lysara. There was a team behind the contact line. Good people. I’d gotten them in and out before.”
He looked down at his hands.
“Korrin got orders to pull out. Evac the planet. The team didn’t make the list. Acceptable losses.”
Sael didn’t interrupt.
“I couldn’t do it,” Mosby said quietly. “I walked into the command post, argued with him, and when that didn’t work…” He shrugged. “I decked him.”
Her eyebrows lifted slightly.
“Took the Star and went back for them. By the time I got there, Korrin had already withdrawn the unit. We were left on Lysara. Me, the team, and a ship that wasn’t supposed to be there.”
The light beyond the canopy shifted again.
“I got us into orbit, found a jump point, and pushed her harder than she was rated for.”
A pause.
“Most of us made it.”
“One of them was hit bad. Autodoc stabilized him twice.” His gaze stayed forward. “There was nowhere left to take him. Support had already redeployed.”
A breath.
“Third time it couldn’t bring him back.”
“He’d had a boy three months earlier. Named him Arthur Caleb.”
Sael did not move.
She understood then why the name mattered.
“I was ordered to leave them,” Mosby said. “That was the calculation.”
His jaw tightened once.
“I don’t like those calculations.”
“Then what?” she asked.
“The war was over by the time we reached a friendly base,” Mosby said. “They were mustering everyone out fast. The depot I processed through was also a collection point. They were scrapping shuttles just as fast.”
He glanced toward the bulkhead.
“I got my pay and saw her sitting in line for the torch. Couldn’t let that happen after all she got me through. I talked the sergeant into marking her surplus, and before anyone looked too closely, I was gone.”
A faint, crooked smile.
“Found out later one of the guys on that team had a father in the Council. Didn’t like his son being written off. That ended Korrin’s career in the regular service.”
A small shrug.
“He found another path.”
Mosby fell silent.
The only sound in the cockpit was the low murmur of life-support and the distant whisper of field stabilizers.
Sael watched him.
He had told her more than he intended.
He did not regret it.
None of that had been in the briefings.
A child would not have appeared in them.
“Well,” Mosby said at last, “I need something to drink. You want anything?”
“No, thank you.”
He unstrapped and left.
It was his watch.
He never left the controls during his watch.
Sael settled back into the seat.
The man she had been assigned was not just a surplus pilot with a stubborn streak. He was someone who disobeyed orders when lives were on the line. Someone who struck a commanding officer and flew into a warzone alone.
That kind of person was not predictable.
That kind of person was not controllable.
She found, to her surprise, that this did not make her feel safer, or entirely uneasy.
It made her curious.
 
Chapter 5
The next day, late in the afternoon, Sael was in the cargo bay checking the restraints on the pallets when Mosby’s voice came over the intercom.
“We’re coming up on the exit. Everything good down there?”
“Everything’s good and tight,” she replied, giving one of the ratchet handles a final tug.
“Alright. You’ll want to get up here.”
Sael wiped her hands on a rag and headed for the gantry. She climbed to the second deck, boots ringing lightly on the metal steps, then moved through the corridor toward the cockpit. Exactly what Mosby wanted.

Ted tilted his head slightly. “Efficiency.”

Dark eyes met his.
A subtle nod.
Work complete.

Ready.

Inside, they prepared to leave it behind.
 

 
Chapter 10

Chapter 11
Silence didn’t arrive with them.

“You can supervise,” Mosby said. “From a padded drawer.”

He didn’t resist when they guided him toward the ladder. The ship rolled again, slow and nauseating; warning lights strobed along the corridor, and somewhere aft a relay popped like a nail snapping under strain.
In the med bay Mosby brought the autodoc online and the lid irised open with patient indifference.
Ted regarded it like an insult given physical form. “I dislike this machine.”
“Yeah,” Mosby said, easing him into place. “It’s not thrilled about you either.”
Sael secured the restraints while Mosby pulled up external feeds and tied them into the autodoc display. “You can still see what we see,” Mosby told him. “Talk me through it.”
“…Very well,” Ted said, and even then, there was a pause, as if he were choosing the least humiliating way to be right. “Friend Mosby… you will need assistance.”
The lid lowered. Pale blue light filled the chamber.
Which left two of them.
Sael sealed into her pressure suit beside the airlock while diagnostics crawled across her visor, one line at a time like a slow confession.
“I have not performed external repair under rotational drift,” she said.
Mosby checked her tether himself — not because he didn’t trust her, but because the universe had already proven it didn’t care. “You’ve done worse.”
“I have not.”
“Then today’s your day.”
The outer hatch opened.
Vacuum took the sound away at once, but it couldn’t take the motion. Stars slid in a slow, sickening curve as the Farther Star turned beneath them; each step with the mag boots was a negotiation with inertia, each movement something that had to be paid for in careful, controlled thrust.
In the distance, Dawnreach hung pale and shattered.
For a moment Sael forgot the repair. The broken ring held her attention the way a wound holds the eye; her mind kept trying to complete the missing arc, to make it whole, to pretend it hadn’t happened. Around it, wreckage drifted in slow, obscene grace, catching sunlight in dull flashes.
Her visor magnified a debris cluster without being asked.
She let it.
The liner’s hull rolled through the frame, a piece of it anyway — too cleanly cut, too recognizably civilian, too full of ghosts she could almost see through the metal.
Through the torn plating she glimpsed a corridor lined with cabin doors, still closed, still numbered. One overhead light blinked on and off with stubborn regularity, illuminating a strip of carpet that looked untouched by vacuum.
Beyond it, something round and white drifted lazily across the passage — a service cart, its wheels turning in slow, meaningless revolution.
“Sael,” Mosby’s voice said in her ear.
She didn’t answer.
“Eyes on the job.”
She blinked and the magnification collapsed. “On it.”
They worked deliberately: clamping the damaged vane, cutting away twisted conduit, securing a temporary brace while the ship continued its slow roll beneath them, as if it were trying to shake off the memory of what had touched it.
A warped panel came free as Mosby cut the final bolt.
“I’ve got it,” he said.
He didn’t.
The panel slipped, caught his thruster pack, and yanked him off the hull in a clean, ugly jerk that turned him into a swinging weight on his tether.
“Caleb!” Sael shouted, and the name came out of her without permission.
He swung outward, rotation turning him into a pendulum. “Thrusters misaligned! Can’t counter!”
Her training rose cleanly: secure debris, prevent further damage, do not compound the emergency.
Instead, she pushed off.
Short bursts. Hard angle. No elegance — only timing.
“Sael—don’t—” Mosby began.
She caught his arm. Impact spun them both.
“Brace!” she snapped.
She fired against their shared momentum. It wasn’t perfect, but it was enough. Their lines snapped tight and dragged them back into the ship’s rotation until plating filled her visor; they hit together, magnets catching with a jarring thud that she felt all the way up her teeth.
For a moment neither of them moved.
“You still in there?” Mosby asked, voice rough.
“Yes,” she said, and made it sound like an answer instead of a prayer.
They re-anchored and finished the brace.
When they got back inside, Ted was conscious in the autodoc, his image ghosting on the cockpit display.
“You survived,” he observed.
“Barely,” Mosby muttered.
The comm panel was alive with overlapping emergency traffic. Sael brought up the long-range view again and forced herself to look at what she already knew.
The Dawnreach Gate hung dark. A section of its inner ring was missing. There was no transit glow, no emergence flares, no orderly stream of ships turning into dots and vanishing.
No ships coming through.
“It’s gone,” she said.
“Gate aperture collapsed,” Ted replied. “No further transit possible.”
Mosby leaned back slowly, as if the chair might give him one second of weightless denial. “So, the system’s cut off.”
“Yes. Mass traffic will require sub light or relay.”
Sael pulled up the news band. The headlines were already forming a narrative, as if catastrophe couldn’t exist until it had a ticker.
DAWNREACH GATE CATASTROPHIC FAILURE
EMERGENCY RESPONSE OVERWHELMED
MILITARY ASSETS DEPLOYING TO SECURE TRAFFIC LANES
The feed refreshed.
She froze.
“Mosby…”
A highlighted alert crawled across the channels.
TRADER VESSEL FARTHER STAR WANTED FOR IMMEDIATE DETENTION
CREW BELIEVED TO HAVE BEEN IN CLOSE PROXIMITY TO INITIAL FIELD DISTURBANCE
AUTHORITIES REQUEST ALL STATIONS AND VESSELS REPORT SIGHTINGS
Sael read it aloud, because silence would have made it worse.
From the autodoc bay, Ted paused as if considering the shape of the problem. “That is… inconvenient,” he said.
Mosby exhaled. “We’re not getting a friendly debrief.”
“They think we caused it,” Sael said.
“Or they need someone they can point at,” Mosby replied, and the bitterness in it was old.
Outside, emergency beacons pulsed among drifting wreckage; rescue craft moved in tight patterns that looked disciplined only because the scale was so obscene that anything less would have been meaningless.
Inside the cockpit, the Farther Star sat wounded in the shadow of a broken gate.
They were no longer witnesses.
They were suspects. 
Chapter 12
The Farther Star eased into a slow arc through the outer traffic band, her rotation finally damped to something that resembled flight. Every system readout carried some shade of caution—nothing red, nothing settled. Mosby flexed his fingers over the controls as if reminding himself they still belonged to him.
“We’re not dead,” he said. “That’s step one.”
Sael did not answer. She had the long-range view expanded again—farther than it needed to be—because looking away felt like letting it happen twice. The debris field no longer looked chaotic. It was beginning to organize under shared momentum: clusters drifting into loose families, fragments tracing arcs that would take years to disperse. She tracked one large piece as it moved against the stars. It would clear the primary lanes in weeks. Smaller fragments would not. The wreckage continued to spread, not violently now but steadily, with the patient confidence of physics.
It would not fix itself.
“Where are the rescue lanes forming?” Mosby asked.
Sael widened the overlay. Emergency vectors converged with grim efficiency. Military transponders multiplied by the minute, threading into the traffic like stitching closing a wound. There were not enough of them to stitch it closed.
“They’re locking down the core corridors,” she said. “Any vessel without clearance is being told to power down and wait.”
“Inspection leads to questions,” Mosby said.
“Questions lead to us sitting still,” Ted replied faintly from the autodoc, his voice thinner but still sharp at the edges.
Mosby muted the external comms. The cockpit fell quieter at once, not calmer—just more private. “Alright,” he said. “Hit me.”
Ted’s image flickered onto the display. The autodoc’s light made his features look even more carved than usual, as if the machine was turning him into a diagram.
“There is a facility,” he said carefully. “It does not officially exist. It will not ask questions it cannot afford to hear.”
“A station?” Mosby asked.
“A structure. Former logistics node. It is reachable.”
“Distance?”
“Under standard conditions, several hours.” Ted paused. “Under our current condition… one to two days.”
“Two days in a damaged ship, in a system full of patrols.”
“Patrol density is concentrated within eight thousand kilometers of the debris field,” Ted said. “Peripheral traffic control is minimal. For now.”
Mosby studied the traffic map in silence, letting the answer finish itself. Every active transponder clustered near the wreckage. Military assets were stacking into rescue corridors, carving lanes for medevac and debris sweep. No one was pushing outward. No one was patrolling the margins.
Rescue was the only thing anyone could agree on. Everything else would come later.
If they stayed where the authorities wanted them, they would be found quickly. If they moved now—quietly—they might simply fall below priority.
“And the alternative?” he asked anyway. “We power down and wait to be sorted.”
No one answered.
“Alright,” Mosby said at last. “We go where we’re not supposed to exist.”
He nudged the Star off the main emergency flow into a slower orbital plane, shaving their profile down to something boring. Their transponder shifted to low-priority maintenance status. On the external cameras, hull lights reflected faintly off scarred plating, insignia burned into ghosts. Manny’s paintwork had suffered for it: one of the stylized eyes was gone entirely, and the other had warped into something that looked less like protection and more like warning.
“Plot me a path that looks tired,” Mosby said. “Slow. Broken.”
“I will guide you,” Ted said. “When I lose coherence, follow the highlighted route.”
“Comforting.”
The Farther Star eased into a long, shallow burn. Every vibration traveled through the deck now, and every adjustment carried the subtle protest of stressed metal. Behind them, Dawnreach flickered like a failing star. Ahead, empty space waited—never safe, merely unclaimed.
Sael watched the rescue lanes shrink behind them until they were only lines and cones on an overlay, too clean to match what she’d seen through the glass.
“When we get there,” she said quietly, “what do we tell them?”
Mosby didn’t look at her. “Nothing they didn’t already know.”
“And if the Ascendants are looking for the same place?”
“Then we get there first.”
The Star crawled onward, paint burned, systems strained, three lives and a thousand unanswered questions riding inside her hull.
The cockpit was quieter now, but it wasn’t calm. Mosby trimmed their drift rate a fraction at a time, making them look less like a ship with somewhere to be and more like one trying not to be in anyone’s way. Ted’s guidance returned over the internal channel at intervals—faint but focused, the mind still working even while the body lay strapped under the autodoc’s light.
“Adjust starboard vector point-two degrees. There is a patrol corridor forming along your current projection.”
Mosby nudged the controls. “Got it.”
On the edge of the scope, a new string of military transponders blinked into being, sliding into organized positions around the wreckage zone. Rescue had become security.
Sael saw it too. “They’re locking it down.”
“Yeah,” Mosby said. “And we don’t want to be inside the lock.”
The Star rolled gently, altering course by a margin small enough that no automated traffic system would flag it as evasive. Just another tired freighter correcting drift. Inside, the ship felt smaller as Mosby powered down noncritical sections at Ted’s prompting. Lights faded aft. The Farther Star shrank into herself to conserve strength.
“We should rest in shifts,” Sael said.
“Soon,” Mosby replied. “Let’s get clear of the main sensor cones first.”
Minutes stretched into an hour. The system’s inner glow dimmed behind them as they climbed into a higher orbital band, traffic thinning to scattered long-haul freighters and automated tankers running deep loops. Ted’s voice came less frequently now, but it came.
“Vector correction in six minutes.”
“Reduce thrust for thermal balance.”
“Maintain attitude. Do not yaw further.”
Sael studied the starfield ahead, the emptier dark that always looked honest until you learned what lived in it. “How do you know this place still exists?” she asked.
A pause, long enough to feel like pain.
“Because things like it rarely die,” Ted said. “They become… unofficial.”
Mosby snorted. “That’s one word for it.”
Another quiet stretch passed—quiet the way a room is quiet when everyone is listening to the same distant noise and pretending, they aren’t. Sael’s hands rested on the console exactly where they always did—neat, controlled, professional—but they weren’t doing anything. Not even hovering.
“When I saw the liner,” she said suddenly.
Both of them turned.
“I recognized it,” she went on, and her voice stayed level in a way that didn’t quite hold. “The configuration. The window bands. I’ve been on ships like that. Families take those routes. Children.”
She swallowed once, a small, tight motion.
“I keep thinking… if we’d been ten seconds slower. If Mosby hadn’t pushed the Star the way he did. If Ted hadn’t felt the pattern before the instruments did—” Her voice faltered. Not broke. Faltered. “And I keep seeing the windows,” she finished. “Not when it broke. Before. When it was still a ship.”
Then she stopped talking. Not as a choice. As if the sentence had simply run out of strength.
For a long moment no one filled the space. The Farther Star drifted on, systems whispering, hull still radiating residual heat. Outside, the broken geometry of the gate had faded into distance, but not into irrelevance.
Mosby didn’t rush her. He rested one hand on the console edge, grounding himself before he spoke.
“You didn’t cause that,” he said—not gentle, not harsh. Certain.
Sael shook her head once. “I was part of what brought us there.”
“So was I,” Mosby said. “So was Ted. So were a thousand captains who trusted that gate.”
The intercom clicked softly before Ted spoke. “Responsibility is not the same as causation,” he said. “They diverge more often than most people are comfortable admitting.”
Sael let out a breath she’d been holding since the corridor came apart. It wasn’t a sob. It wasn’t even loud.
It was just… tired.

For a while after that, no one spoke.
The Farther Star held her shallow burn, limping outward in a profile so unremarkable it bordered on invisible. Behind them, Dawnreach continued to flicker against the inner system like a wound that refused to close. Patrol geometries tightened. Rescue lanes hardened into cordons. The official story would already be forming.  Most of the patrol craft remained close to the wreckage, clustering around medevac corridors and debris sweep vectors. No one was pushing outward into the margins yet. There were not enough ships for that.
Ahead, the stars thinned.
Somewhere in that dark lay a structure that did not officially exist—an old logistics node turned unofficial harbor, if Ted’s memory and hope were still aligned. If central command remembered it at all, it would be as a decommissioned entry buried in procurement archives, flagged inactive and low priority. Today, every active asset was pointed inward. No one was scanning the margins for relics. It would not appear on public traffic maps. It would not advertise docking clearance. It would not welcome trouble.
And they were bringing trouble with them.  Mosby kept his eyes on the forward display, hands steady on the controls as the Star crept deeper into unclaimed space. Whatever waited at the end of that slow burn—repair, refuge, or another trap—they would meet it without a beacon and without permission.
The Farther Star slipped farther from the light.

 
Chapter 13
Ahead, a faint blip appeared on the long-range scope — too small for a station, too stationary for debris.
Mosby leaned forward. “That it?”
Ted took a moment to answer. “Yes,” he said. “That is it.”
The facility resolved slowly as they closed distance — not a gleaming port, not a beacon of traffic, but a dark lattice of old trusses and mismatched modules tucked into the shadow of a dead comm buoy. No running lights. No broadcast.
Just presence.
Sael watched it grow on the display, shoulders drawn slightly inward now, as if bracing against something she could finally feel. “This is where we’re going to hide?” she asked.
“For a little while,” Mosby said.
The Farther Star crawled toward the forgotten structure on thrusters alone, her scarred hull reflecting faint starlight while her systems whispered their strain and bled fuel into the void. The station didn’t look abandoned. It looked tired. Long docking arms jutted from a central spindle like broken fingers. Sections of the outer ring were dark; hull plating patched in mismatched panels that didn’t quite line up. Old company markings — gas mining consortium logos, Kestrel-Morrow among them, half-burned away — ghosted the metal in pale scars. A few lights burned. Not many. Even those looked like they were lit out of habit, not confidence.
“Getting transponder pings,” Sael said, leaning over her display. Her voice was steadier now. Not because the weight was gone — but because she had accepted it. “Short range. Low power. Civilian. Two outbound contacts,” she added. “Small mass. Industrial drives. They’re accelerating toward the inner lanes.”
Mosby watched the vectors update. The signatures were old and overworked — salvage tugs, not patrol craft.
“Kelpoint’s sending help,” he said.
“Yes,” Ted replied. “Which means they have fewer hands to question us.”
Mosby nodded. “Good.”
Ted’s voice came again, not through the intercom this time but from behind them in the cockpit, quieter now. “Tired places often understand tired ships.”
Sael didn’t reply. But for the first time since the gate, her hands moved again — purposeful, precise — and that mattered more than anything she could have said.
Mosby muttered, more to himself than either of them, “Good. Means someone’s home.”
Ted leaned forward, eyes narrowed at the passive returns. “They are observing us. No weapons lock. No organized traffic control.”
“Translation?” Mosby asked.
“They are deciding whether we are trouble.”
Mosby huffed softly. “Fair.”
He eased the Star closer, fighting the slow tumble still lurking in her mass distribution. The ship answered sluggishly, attitude jets firing in uneven pulses; every correction cost fuel they didn’t really have. He flew like a man balancing a cup he couldn’t afford to spill.
“Open channel,” he said.
Sael keyed the transmitter. “Station traffic, this is independent trader Farther Star. We’re in need of temporary berth and minor assistance. No hazardous cargo. No passengers. We can compensate with supplies or services.”
Static filled the cockpit for a long breath.
Then a voice came through, older, roughened by recycled air and long years. “Trader, you look like you got into a fight with a gate and lost.”
Mosby allowed himself the faintest smile. “Feels about right.”
A pause. Quiet voices in the background.
“You armed?” the voice asked.
“Sidearms,” Mosby said. “Standard. Nothing heavy.”
Another pause. Longer this time.
“You leak anything that’ll poison us?”
“Not if I can help it,” Mosby said. “We’re nursing a bad fuel tank. That’s about it.”
More muffled discussion.
Finally: “Docking arm six. Slow. Real slow. We don’t have the parts to fix what you break.”
Mosby let out a breath he hadn’t realized he’d been holding. “Appreciate it.”
“Don’t,” the voice said. “We’re not doing this for you.”
The channel cut.
Sael glanced sideways at Mosby. “Encouraging.”
“Means they’re honest,” he said. “I’ll take that.”
Docking arm six extended in fits and starts, guidance lights flickering uncertainly. It took two attempts to get the alignment right. On the third, the docking collar met the Star’s scarred flank with a dull, hollow clunk that traveled through the hull like a tired concession.
Seals took longer than they should have. When the pressure light finally shifted from amber to green, the cockpit felt quieter than it had since the disaster.
“Atmosphere on the other side,” Sael said.
Ted lowered himself carefully into the copilot’s seat, movements stiff. “I would prefer not to return to the autodoc immediately,” he said.
Mosby shot him a look. “You just got stitched back together.”
“Yes,” Ted said. “Which is why I would like to remain that way.”
Sael almost smiled despite everything. “You’re not walking alone.”
“I am not,” Ted agreed.
Mosby powered down non-essentials, leaving the Star on a low, watchful hum. For a moment he just sat there, hands resting on the controls, as if the stillness itself were something he did not entirely trust. Then he stood.
“Alright,” he said quietly. “Let’s go meet the neighbors.”
The cargo ramp lowered with a tired whine. Cold station air spilled into the bay — dry, metallic, carrying the faint smell of old machinery and recycled life support. Not unpleasant. Just… worn. It smelled like something that had been repaired too many times and still hadn’t been forgiven.
They stepped down together.
They weren’t met by guards.
They were met by people.
A loose semicircle had formed beyond the docking collar — two dozen figures in patched suits and work clothes, some with tools still in their hands, others leaning on crates or railings. Thin faces. Weathered expressions. Eyes that had learned to measure strangers fast.
No one spoke at first.
They took in the Star’s scorched hull, the missing paint, the scar along her flank where metal had been replaced with something that only vaguely resembled original plating. Then they looked at Mosby, Sael, and Ted, and Sael felt the assessment settle on her skin like grit.
She saw the children first — only three of them — standing close behind an older woman with a respirator hanging loose around her neck. One of the kids stared openly at the Star like it was a myth come to life.
Mosby raised one empty hand, slow and visible. “Afternoon,” he said. “Didn’t mean to drop in uninvited. We’re running on fumes and bad luck. Figured we’d try knocking instead of drifting.”
A murmur passed through the group — not unfriendly, not welcoming. Just… measuring.
Then someone stepped forward.
He was broad-shouldered once, though time had taken some of that. Gray threaded through his beard, and the patch on his sleeve still bore the faded insignia of a gas extraction company long gone. His left leg moved stiffly, old injury or bad surgery. Authority without a uniform.
He stopped ten meters from them and planted his boots.
“I’m Hardin,” he said. “Welcome to Kelpoint Processing Station. Or what’s left of it.”
His voice was steady, not loud — the kind of voice used to being listened to in small, serious rooms. “This station doesn’t get visitors unless something’s gone very wrong.”
Mosby nodded once. “Mosby. Captain of the Farther Star. And yeah… that sounds about right.”
Hardin’s eyes flicked to Ted, then Sael, then back to Mosby. “You armed?”
“Sidearms on the ship,” Mosby said. “Didn’t seem polite to come out carrying.”
That earned the faintest shift in the crowd — tension easing by a hair.
Hardin’s gaze lingered on the Star again. “You look like you came through the gate mess.”
Mosby didn’t answer immediately. “We were in the neighborhood,” he said at last.
Hardin held his eyes, weighing the half-truth, then looked past them toward the open cargo bay. “What do you want?”
“Time,” Mosby said. “Docking. Maybe a few parts if you’ve got scrap that still remembers what it used to be. We can trade.”
Hardin’s expression didn’t change. “Trade what?”
“Food,” Mosby said first. “Nothing fancy. But it’s sealed, shelf-stable, and not protein paste pretending to be something else.” He jerked a thumb back toward the Star. “Medical consumables. Filters. Nutrient packs. A few crates of things that are small enough to be ignored on a manifest and valuable enough to matter when you don’t have them.”
A murmur passed through the group — quieter this time, sharper. Not hope. Calculation.
“Also work,” Mosby added. “We fix things. Haul things. Lift heavy stuff that doesn’t want to move anymore. And we don’t ask why it broke in the first place.”
A few heads tilted at that. Practical. Useful. Still not enough. People here already knew how to work. They’d been doing it without help for a long time, and the kinds of help that arrived from outside usually came with strings.
Sael wasn’t looking at Hardin anymore.
She was looking past him.
At the woman with the loose respirator whose hands shook when she adjusted the seal. At a man with a collarbone that had healed wrong and never been corrected. At a boy trying very hard not to limp. At the gray in too many faces that should still have had color.
Twenty-seven people, she counted without meaning to.
Maybe fewer in full health.
This wasn’t a crew.
This was what was left.
“We have an autodoc,” she said.
The word didn’t echo.
It moved.
Like a pressure change. Like something everyone felt at once and no one wanted to believe too quickly.
Hardin’s eyes snapped back to her. “You actually mean that?”
“Full diagnostic and treatment capacity,” Sael said, voice steady because it had to be. “Surgical assist. Infection control. Bone alignment. Internal repair. We don’t have infinite supplies—” she didn’t soften that, didn’t dress it up “—but we can help.”
She heard herself say it and understood, with a calm that felt almost separate from her body, what she was spending. Each treatment would be a choice; each choice would make leaving easier for them and harder for her. And once you started healing strangers, you didn’t get to pretend you’d only stopped for fuel.
Someone inhaled sharply behind Hardin. The woman with the respirator took a step forward before she seemed to realize she had moved.
Hardin didn’t look at her. He kept his eyes on Sael, searching now, not for leverage, but for cost.
“You’d do that,” he said. Not a question. A test.
Sael nodded once. “Yes.”
Mosby glanced at her, surprised, but not objecting. He’d seen that look before — the one that had nothing to do with orders.
Hardin studied the three of them for a long moment. Then he jerked his chin toward the ship.
“Let’s see who needs that machine of yours first.”
He paused, eyes narrowing slightly.
“And if this is a lie,” he added, letting the words hang between them.
Mosby gave a short nod. “Fair.”
The crowd didn’t part so much as shift, a narrow path opening through patched coats and worn boots.
That was when Ted went very still.
Sael felt it beside her — not fear, not pain.
Recognition.
From near the back of the group, someone moved forward slowly, leaning on a narrow metal cane that had been repaired so many times it was more weld than original structure.
She was Talari.
Age showed differently on her kind, but it was there all the same — in the slight tremor of her upper limbs, in the faded vibrancy of the markings along her throat and hands. The patterns, once sharp and intricate, had softened with time, like ink left too long beneath unforgiving suns.
Her eyes fixed on Ted.
Not curious.
Hungry.
Ted stepped forward before Mosby or Sael could speak.
“Elder,” he said softly.
His voice had changed. The cadence was deeper, the resonance fuller — formal in a way neither of them had ever heard from him before.
The old Talari made a faint sound, somewhere between a breath and a laugh. “It has been a long time,” she said. Her voice carried a subtle harmonic underlayer, the sound of someone unused to speaking her own language aloud. “I had begun to think I would not see another of my people again.”
Ted inclined his head, a gesture Sael now recognized as respect.
“I am—”
He spoke his name in full, the sound hanging in the air like a remembered chord.
“But Ted will serve,” he added. “I did not know anyone remained here.”
“Nor did I,” she replied.
Her gaze shifted briefly to Mosby and Sael, then back to Ted. Something in her posture eased — not fully, but enough.
She turned her head slightly toward Hardin.
“They are not here to take,” she said. “They are… frayed. Like us.”
Hardin studied her for a moment, then nodded once. Whatever history lay between them, he trusted her judgment.
“Alright,” he said. “We start with the sick and the broken. Then we talk trade.”
Sael exhaled quietly. She hadn’t realized how tight her chest had been.
Ted stepped closer to the old Talari, lowering his voice. “You have been without exchange,” he said gently.
“For too long,” she replied.
Sael didn’t understand the full meaning, but she saw what it did to Ted — the subtle straightening, the return of a steadiness he’d been forcing since the disaster. For the first time since the gate collapsed, he didn’t look alone.
The old Talari studied him another moment, then inclined her head with quiet formality.
“My name is Rheva,” she said, using the simplified cadence meant for human tongues. “Once of the Third Weft. Now… of here.”
The last words held no self-pity.
Only fact.
“Ted,” Mosby said gently, stepping in beside them, “you wanna translate that for us, or is this one of those we’ll explain later moments?”
Ted kept his gaze on Rheva. “It means she has been separated from her people for a very long time,” he said. “Long enough that the patterns of shared memory… fade.”
Rheva’s eyes softened slightly. “We do not do well alone,” she said. “We remember together.”
Sael glanced between them, something tightening in her throat. “You haven’t seen another Talari in all this time?”
Rheva made a small motion that might have been a shrug. “Ships pass. They do not stop. Or they stop, and leave quickly.”
Her gaze flicked to the Farther Star’s scarred hull. “You look like you do not have the luxury of leaving quickly.”
A faint ripple of dry humor moved through the nearby listeners. Even Hardin’s mouth twitched.
Mosby spread his hands. “We specialize in poor timing.”
Hardin grunted. “Good. You’ll fit right in.”
He turned toward the inner corridor. “Autodoc first. Then we talk about what you need — and what we can spare.”
Rheva lingered beside Ted as the others began to move.
“You carry noise,” she said quietly, eyes half-lidded. “Pain. Shock. But also… threads that do not belong only to you.”
Ted went still for a fraction of a second, then inclined his head.
“Yes,” he said simply.
She studied him once more, then nodded as if confirming something to herself.
“Good,” she said. “Then perhaps we are not finished yet.”
Sael didn’t know what that meant, but she felt it settle into the air between them — not hope exactly, but something that hadn’t been there before.
Hardin clapped his hands once, the sound sharp in the worn corridor.
“Alright,” he said. “If we’re doing this, we do it before my people start pretending they’re not lining up already.”
They were.
Word had spread fast. By the time they reached the docking collar, a loose cluster had formed in the passageway — men and women trying to look casual, older folks leaning on rails, three children pressed close together near the bulkhead. They stood like people waiting for news they’d trained themselves not to expect.
Sael stopped short. “We can’t bring the autodoc out,” she said. “But we have a field scanner.”
Ted paused at the foot of the ramp, breathing a little too carefully. His pride got him halfway up before his ribs reminded him, he was not metal. Sael caught his elbow.
“I’ll get it,” she said.
He started to protest; Mosby cut him off with a look.
Sael went up into the Star and returned a moment later with a hardened case about the size of a small suitcase, setting it down carefully on a cargo crate. The lid opened with a soft click, revealing a compact array of articulated sensors and a palm-sized interface panel.
Hardin eyed it. “That thing actually do anything?”
Sael knelt beside it. “Vitals, internal imaging, fracture mapping, infection detection. It tells us who needs real treatment first.”
“And the real treatment?” Hardin asked.
“On the ship,” Mosby said. “One at a time.”
Hardin nodded once. “We’ll keep them moving.”
The first patient was the woman with the respirator. She waved off help, stubborn, until Sael guided her gently to sit on a crate. The scanner’s arms unfolded with a faint mechanical whisper, passing slow bands of light across her chest and neck.
Numbers flickered across the display.
“Chronic lung scarring,” Sael said quietly. “Low oxygen saturation. We can help, but she needs the autodoc.”
Hardin didn’t hesitate. “You heard her,” he said, gesturing to two nearby men. “Careful. Don’t make her regret this.”
Sael directed the men as they helped the woman up the ramp into the Star, moving like they were handling something fragile and valuable.
Mosby looked away.
After that it became a rhythm: scan, decide, move. Some people only needed antibiotics or splints. Those stayed. Others were scheduled by need, and when their turn came, they were escorted up the ramp into the Farther Star’s med-bay. The crowd didn’t talk much. They only watched. Not with gratitude, but with something closer to disbelief — as if the whole arrangement might evaporate if anyone named it too loudly.
A boy went next because his grandmother pushed him forward and refused to meet Sael’s eyes. The scanner hummed softly over his leg.
“Hairline fracture,” Sael said gently. “Old. Healed wrong. That’s why it hurts.”
The grandmother’s hands trembled. “Can you fix it?”
“We can try,” Sael said, and meant it.
Further down the corridor one of the children edged closer, staring at the glowing bands of light like it was something out of a story. Sael caught the look and softened her voice.
“You next,” she said gently.
The kid didn’t smile.
But he stepped forward.
Mosby slipped back onto the ship while the line continued. He returned a few minutes later carrying a sealed case and lowered it onto the deck. The crate had no branding, no slogans, but was clearly marked: food.
No announcement followed. Curious, several came forward and opened it. Someone cracked a package open.
The smell carried — warm protein, preserved vegetables, something spiced enough to feel intentional. Real.
That changed the room. A woman reached out, hesitated, then took one pack and stepped back as if afraid it might vanish if she moved too quickly. Others followed, careful and restrained, people used to scarcity.
Sael watched the food disappear into hands that tried not to shake, and felt the practical trap of it close another notch. You could treat a stranger and still leave; you could feed a starving station and pretend it was only trade, but the memory of it would travel with you either way. And it would travel with them, too — which meant it could be used against the station as easily as it could be remembered as mercy.
Hardin watched it happen, jaw tightening just a little. “You didn’t say you were carrying this.”
Mosby shrugged. “Didn’t think it needed selling.”
Behind them, the low hum inside the Farther Star shifted pitch as external power came online. Indicator lights along the cargo bay bulkhead flickered from amber to steady green.
Ted, standing just inside the ramp now, glanced up toward the ceiling, listening. “They have given us station power,” he said. “Crude, but stable.”
Mosby nodded. “Good. Means we’re not draining what little we’ve got left.”
Ted’s gaze shifted to Sael, then the scanner. “Continue triage,” he said. “I will begin evaluating the damage.”
“You’re supposed to be recovering,” Sael said.
Ted tilted his head slightly. “This is how I recover.”
Rheva made a soft, approving sound beside him. “Purpose is a form of repair.”
Mosby jerked a thumb toward the ramp. “Don’t wander too far. I’d like the ship to still be here when we’re done.”
Ted’s eyes flicked toward the station conduit running along the docking collar. “I will not go far. The ship’s systems are… distressed.”
“That’s one word for it,” Mosby muttered.
Ted moved back up the ramp, one hand trailing along the scarred bulkhead as if reacquainting himself with an old friend. Once inside, he paused at a wall panel and opened it, exposing a tangle of patched conduits and temporary bypasses. He stood there a moment, listening to the Star’s internal rhythms through the metal.
Then he began to work.
Outside, the line of patients thinned slowly. Those needing full treatment disappeared into the ship one at a time, returning later quieter, steadier, disbelief replaced by cautious relief. Hardin watched it all with his arms folded, jaw tight.
“You keep this up,” he said quietly to Mosby, “my people are going to start thinking you’re the best thing that’s happened to this station in ten years.”
Mosby didn’t look at him. “We’re feeling the same about you folks here.”
Hardin huffed, not quite a laugh. “That’s because you haven’t tried to fix our water recyclers yet.”
Mosby glanced toward the open ramp, where another patient was being helped inside. “Give us a day,” he said. “We’ll disappoint you.”
Hardin’s expression softened by a fraction. “Good,” he said. “Wouldn’t trust you if you didn’t.”
He pushed off the bulkhead and turned back toward the remaining cluster of watchers. “Alright, that’s enough standing around. If you’re not sick, you’re working. Same as always.”
The corridor began to break apart, motion returning in slow, practical currents. Tools got picked up. Panels opened. Someone dragged a cart of scrap past the docking arm. Somewhere deeper in the station a motor kicked, coughed, and decided — grudgingly — to keep living.
Over the next hour the Star settled deeper into her berth, umbilicals locking home with tired clanks as station power bled into her systems. The hum aboard shifted — less strained, less desperate — like a runner finally allowed to lean against a wall.
Ted was already half-disassembled into the guts of engineering when Mosby climbed down from the cockpit.
“Dockside power is dirty,” Ted called without looking up. “But within tolerance. I am rebalancing load so we do not cook anything important.”
“Appreciated,” Mosby said. “How bad are we?”
A pause. Tools clinked.
“We are alive,” Ted replied. “Which is not the same thing.”
Mosby snorted softly. “Story of the week.”
Hardin was waiting near docking arm six, leaning against a battered tug whose paint had once been orange and now mostly remembered the idea of it. Two stationers stood with him — already suited, already tired, already committed.
Mosby nodded toward them. “Where you need me?”
“Salvage tug is going out,” one of the stationers said. “With what happened we have more work than people. We have to get what we can while we can.”
Mosby nodded once. He didn’t look back at the ramp; he didn’t trust himself to. He followed Hardin toward the airlock.
“You ever run recovery?” Hardin asked without preamble.
Mosby looked the tug over. External grapples. Patchwork thrusters. A cockpit blister that had been replaced with something from an entirely different ship class.
“Not pretty recovery,” Mosby said. “But yeah.”
Hardin jerked his head toward the airlock. “We’ve got word of a shuttle still drifting from the gate mess. Life signs faded, but not gone last sweep. Tug’s slow, but she’ll get there.”
Mosby didn’t hesitate. “Let’s go.”
The tug smelled like old coolant and recycled air that had given up trying to be fresh. Two other stationers were already strapped in — a woman with oil-blackened hands and a teenager whose boots didn’t quite fit.
They didn’t ask Mosby who he was. They just handed him a tether line and pointed at a console that had to be coaxed into life with a slap.
Out in the debris field, the stars were broken by drifting wreckage — twisted cargo frames, fragments of hull plating.
A length of decorative promenade railing drifted past the viewport, still polished on one side. Someone had cared how it looked.
The shuttle came into view half an hour later, one side peeled open.
“Easy,” Mosby murmured, guiding the tug in while the others worked the grapples.
Inside, they found three survivors.
One conscious. Two not.
Nobody cheered when they sealed the hatch and turned back for the station.
They just worked.
Ted didn’t look up. “They have already asked.”
“Not with words,” Rheva said. “With eyes.”
Ted’s claws paused on a coupling. He inclined his head, the smallest admission.
Rheva’s gaze drifted to the station conduit beyond the docking collar. The insulation was old. The patchwork repairs were not mistakes; they were survival.
“Kelpoint is thin,” she said. “If certain systems fail, there will be no second attempt.”
Ted’s eye ridges twitched. “Which systems.”
Rheva made a faint sound that might have been humor, if there had been any ease left in it. “The ones that keep breath in lungs and water in pipes.”
Ted closed the panel he was in, not with resignation, but with decision. “If Hardin permits.”
Rheva’s markings shifted subtly as she turned. “He already has.”
Ted went to Hardin anyway, because that was what respect looked like when you meant it. Hardin listened without changing expression, then nodded once, the nod of a man who did not waste words when agreement was already the only sensible thing left.
“Do what you can,” he said. “Don’t break what we can’t replace.”
Ted inclined his head. “I will be careful.”
Hardin’s gaze flicked, briefly, toward the Farther Star. “You already have been.”
Ted worked for sixteen straight hours.
Station schematics were a palimpsest of dead corporations and stubborn survivors — original layouts overwritten by a decade of improvisation. He patched a failing life-support recycler in one compartment, rerouted power around a burned conduit in another, rebuilt a magnetic latch assembly using three parts that had never belonged together and one that had been holding a door closed with hope.
Rheva remained nearby, watching in a silence that wasn’t quite silence — low clicks and subharmonic tones passing between them in Talari cadence. Not instruction. Not supervision.
Witness.
By the time Ted turned his attention back to the Farther Star, the station cycle lights had dimmed twice.
He opened the fuel diagnostics out of habit, not concern.
The Helion Salt registers were clean. Containment stable. No radiation bloom. No contamination residue from the overburn.
The Kryseum system feeding the maneuvering drives was not as fortunate.
Ted traced the diagnostics along the fuel cells—where the Kryseum rested in its dense, obedient state until energized, when magnetic fields coaxed it into flow—and followed the distribution paths toward the port thruster cluster. Halfway through the trace his hands slowed, then stopped, as if the interface had resisted him.
The containment mesh showed fractures.
Not a gross breach. Not yet. A hairline discontinuity along the edge of a magnetic seam, the kind of defect a system could live with right up until the moment it could not. The stabilization coils were already compensating, flexing in constant micro-adjustments too small to trip alarms and too patient to be mistaken for danger.
But the defect was real, and it was not sealed.
Under load, Kryseum would begin to vaporize at the margins, bleeding out in quantities the system would insist were tolerable. Ted knew better. He pulled the projected stress curves forward—hard maneuvering, evasive thrust, atmospheric insertion—and watched the compensations multiply until the graph became less a line than a slow insistence toward failure.
In vacuum it would be inconvenient: loss of efficiency, reduced control, a ship that no longer answered cleanly. In atmosphere it would be catastrophic, because there was no graceful way for that kind of blue fire to fail.
Ted ran the analysis again. Then again, stripping assumptions, isolating variables, forcing the system to justify itself as if it might be shamed into honesty.
The answer did not change.
When Mosby returned from salvage—exhausted, suit streaked with vacuum dust and old paint—Ted was waiting at the foot of the ramp. He had cleaned his hands. He had not cleaned the look in his eyes.
“We have a complication,” Ted said.
Mosby closed his eyes for a moment. Not in frustration. In preparation.
“Of course we do.”
“The Kryseum tank feeding the port maneuvering cluster has developed microfractures in its field containment,” Ted said. “It will function for controlled thrust in deep space.”
Mosby opened his eyes. “Define controlled.”
Ted’s eye ridges twitched. “We can reach Ael-Ruun.”
“And after that?”
Ted did not answer immediately, and in that pause Mosby heard what the words had not yet said. He exhaled slowly.
“Say it.”
Ted’s voice stayed quiet, careful. “Blue burns quietly,” he said, “until it does not.”
Mosby nodded once. “So, we might get there.”
“Yes.”
“But lifting off again…”
Ted inclined his head. “Uncertain in atmosphere. Probability of catastrophic failure is high.”
Sael, standing just inside the cargo bay hatch, asked the question the way she might have asked about spare parts. “Can we fix it?”
“The containment tank requires facilities not present,” Ted said. “A complete replacement would be preferable.”
“Could we add another tank?” Sael asked. “Carry it in the cargo hold. Use that instead.”
Ted returned his gaze to the diagnostics, as if the machine itself deserved the courtesy of his refusal. “I would prefer not to die inside a solvent vapor cloud.”
Mosby scratched the back of his neck. “Fair.”
Hardin, who had been listening from a short distance away, shifted his weight, as if deciding whether to offer help or simply admit the shape of the world.
“We don’t carry them inside,” he said. “Not if we can help it.”
They all looked at him.
“When our tugs run long-range salvage,” Hardin continued, “sometimes farther than they were ever meant to go, we bolt an auxiliary Kryseum tank to the outside. Separate containment. Independent feed.” He gestured vaguely, like someone who had welded the solution together more than once. “Transfer line runs external. Ugly. Slow. But it keeps the bad stuff out of the hull.”
Ted turned slightly now, attention fully engaged. “You isolate the flow until transfer?”
“Always,” Hardin said. “Vacuum does the forgiving. Hulls don’t.”
Mosby exhaled through his nose. “You’re saying we could carry fuel without carrying the risk.”
Hardin nodded once. “You’d still be gambling,” he said. “Just not inside your ship.”
Silence followed—not doubt, but calculation.
Ted inclined his head. “This reduces the probability of immediate catastrophic failure,” he said. “It does not remove it.”
Mosby glanced at Sael. “Story of our week.”
Sael didn’t smile. “We wouldn’t be stealth anymore. Not like this.”
“No,” Mosby agreed quietly. “Not even close.” He looked back at the schematic, at the ghosted outline of external tanks riding the Star’s spine. “We’d have to fly careful. Stay out of the main lanes. Keep the tanks pointed away from anyone who might take an interest.”
Hardin snorted softly. “Anything bolted outside a ship looks like something worth asking questions about.”
“I don’t think we have a choice,” Mosby said. “Can we take a look at these tanks?”
The tanks were attached outside a neglected maintenance spur—massive industrial cylinders that had once fed mining rigs large enough to make asteroids flinch. The pumps and piping were a hodgepodge of salvage parts and improvisation.
“Ugly,” Mosby said. “But they’ll hold.”
Ted studied the tank’s structure, eyes unfocusing as he modeled stress paths and field interactions. “Three would suffice,” he said. “Four would provide margin.”
“And how do we fill them?” Mosby asked.
Hardin jerked his chin toward the outer lanes. “Couple of tugs. They’re already running rescue and cleanup. Nobody questions a ship dragging wreckage and bodies.”
He paused, then added, “They make one more stop at a depot on the way back. Bring what they can. Bolt these on and pray nobody looks too hard.”
Sael stared at the tanks. She could already see the silhouette of the ship they would become — less a trader now, more a question mark moving through space with its answer strapped to the outside. And she could see the part no one was saying: if the patrols decided to look hard, Kelpoint would pay for it first.
“That’s… insane,” she said.
Hardin gave her a sideways look. “Welcome to survival.”
Ted’s claws traced an invisible schematic in the air. “We can strap them along the dorsal spine and aft support trusses,” he said. “Tie them into a staged transfer system. Pump the Kryseum over gradually to the starboard tank once we reach Ael-Ruun.”
Mosby grimaced. “Ugly.”
“Dangerous,” Ted added.
Hardin nodded once. “Possible.”
That was enough.
So, they began preparing.

 
Chapter 14
Hardin was standing over a crate of stripped avionics when the stars changed. He would remember later—annoyingly, vividly—that he’d been thinking about nothing larger than a guidance wafer and the cost of quantite.
He had a grease pencil in one hand and a half-cleaned circuit board in the other, arguing with Lira about whether the guidance wafer could be salvaged or if it was only worth its lattice-grown quantite substrate. Around them, the old logistics hub lived the way it always did—slow rotation humming through the deck plating, someone in the galley corner reheating ration bricks salvaged from a cracked freighter, two welders arguing about the bracket Ted had reinforced the night before and whether it would hold another season of stress cycles. It was the kind of argument people had when they were determined to pretend the universe wasn’t still breaking outside the hull.
The “ops board” wasn’t a room. It was a section of bulkhead near the long eating tables where someone had bolted three mismatched display panels and wired them into the original sensor spine the company had abandoned when it pulled out years ago. The spine still worked—mostly. It showed traffic drift, salvage returns, hull integrity on Dock Two, and a running tally of parts worth selling once the markets stabilized. Most days, it also showed the quiet lie every station tells itself: that the next hour will look enough like the last to be survivable.
Prices were down. That much was obvious. There was too much wreckage in orbit and too many hands pulling from it. But wreckage meant supplies—filters, regulators, intact storage cells, the occasional sealed med locker. Hardin was already thinking about what he could convince Ted to look at next. The Talari had added more life to this station in two weeks than it had seen in two years. Pumps that had been decorative now moved fluid. A cracked manifold that everyone had stepped around for months was now pressurized and humming. A place everyone had called dead had begun, stubbornly, to behave like it intended to continue.
He turned toward the board to mark the intake from Tug Three. He leaned in a little, squinting at the numbers the way a man squints at a future he doesn’t trust.
That was when he saw it.
At first, he thought one of the panels had glitched. The starfield behind the docking arm cameras shimmered for a fraction of a second, like heat rising from fresh welds. The sort of distortion you got when optics were dirty or software was tired—or when somebody on the station had bumped the wrong conduit and everything, briefly, forgot which way was up.
Then there was hull where there hadn’t been hull.
No approach vector. No flare. No deceleration burn.
Just mass.
Someone at the long table stopped mid-sentence. The galley unit chimed as if offended at being ignored. One of the welders swore softly. The station itself seemed to pause—rotation, breath, habit—like a man who’s heard his own name spoken behind him in a voice he doesn’t recognize.
Hardin didn’t move. His body had learned something in the war years before the hub was abandoned: motion can be interpreted as invitation.
The external cameras tried to focus. The image sharpened and blurred in uneven pulses, like the optics couldn’t quite decide where the ship began. It was narrow, dark, almost elegant—if you liked your elegance predatory. Not patched. Not salvaged. Not cobbled together from three registries like everything else in this system. It had that smooth inevitability of a thing that had never been desperate.
Intact.
Weapon housings slid open along its flanks with mechanical precision. Not fast. Not dramatic. Just… ready. Like someone rolling up their sleeves without ever raising their voice.
The improvised sensor array stuttered. The classification script spat out UNKNOWN CONTACT and then tried to assign a transponder ID. It found nothing. No ping. No handshake. No broadcast challenge. The station offered its tired little questions and got back only silence—polite, absolute, and insulting.
The ship did not hail.
It did not drift.
It held position beyond the docking arm as if it had always owned that patch of vacuum. As if the laws of motion were something it leased, not something it obeyed.
Behind Hardin, someone whispered, “That’s not salvage.”
No one laughed.
Hardin felt it in his gut before he let himself think it: this wasn’t random traffic. This wasn’t patrol. This was about the freighter docked two rings over with external tanks waiting to be bolted on and a Talari engineer who had already made too many things work. Trouble didn’t come this clean unless it had been called. And Hardin hadn’t called it—at least not with words.
He set the circuit board down very carefully. He did it the way you set down something that might break and cut you if you drop it wrong.
“Get Mosby,” he said. “And the Star’s crew. Now.”
He did the math the way he always did—field output versus their nonexistent shields, power density versus their patched reactors, weapon geometry versus a station built out of leftovers. Not hero math. Survival math. The kind that never comes out even.
Twenty-seven souls.
Two salvage tugs currently out, hauling fuel tanks meant for the Star.
One crew member in the autodoc, nearly finished with treatment.
One freighter docked and half-fitted for a long run into empty space.
Hands that were very good at improvisation and very bad at fighting warships. A place built for endurance, not confrontation.
“Close the outer salvage doors,” he said quietly. “Bring Tug Three in if they’re within burn range. No panic calls.” If panic got into the vents, it would spread faster than fire.
“Should we challenge?” Lira asked.
Hardin looked at the display again.
The ship was close enough that he could see the way its hull swallowed the station lights. Close enough that if it fired, there would be no second decision to make. A clean shot would turn this whole ring into particulate and opinions.
“Challenge what?” he said, not unkindly. The question was almost gentle. The answer was anything but.
The wall display blinked.
A docking request appeared in their system.
Not broadcast. Not transmitted across open bands. It simply populated the queue with the correct authorization tags, formatted to the station’s outdated protocol stack like it had been written from the inside. Whoever sent it knew exactly which modules the company had disabled before leaving—and which ones Hardin had re-enabled to keep the place alive. It wasn’t hacking. It was familiarity. Like a stranger walking into your kitchen and reaching for the right drawer without looking.
Hardin felt something cold settle behind his ribs. Not fear, exactly—fear was loud. This was the quieter realization that your walls are symbolic.
They hadn’t been scanned loudly.
They’d been read. Not measured. Not cataloged. Read—like a file. Like a confession.
He glanced around the room—the tables, the patched bulkheads, the half-eaten meal trays, the salvage bins. This wasn’t a military outpost. It wasn’t even a real station anymore. It was a group of tired people making something livable out of catastrophe. That was the crime, apparently. Survival without permission.
If he refused, what happened next would not be a negotiation. Negotiation required shared assumptions. This thing had brought its own.
“Cycle Dock Two,” he said.
Dock Two was the farthest working berth from the habitation cluster. The longest walk. The most bulkheads between the corvette and the people who actually lived here. Distance wasn’t safety, but it was time—and time was all Hardin ever had to spend.
A few of the others looked at him.
“You’re letting it in?” someone asked.
“I’m not letting it do anything,” Hardin replied. “I’m making sure it doesn’t decide to.” He heard his own voice and hated how steady it sounded, like he was already negotiating terms with gravity.
Outside, the docking arm extended. Its hydraulics whined louder than usual, embarrassed by their own age. The corvette adjusted by a fraction—no visible thrusters, no corrective jets—and slid into alignment with uncanny precision. It moved the way a thought moves when it’s certain it’s right.
The clamps engaged.
The vibration traveled through the deck plating and into the soles of Hardin’s boots. It didn’t feel like docking. It felt like a lock turning.
Only then did someone in the galley remember to breathe.
Footsteps pounded down the corridor behind him.
Mosby came in first, still in work boots, jaw tight. Sael a half-step behind followed by Ted. Ted moved like his body had agreed to obey only because his mind required it.
“What’s up—” Mosby began.
He saw the screen.
“Oh.”
No one needed to explain it.
Hardin kept his eyes on the camera feed as the dark hull settled against patched metal. Whatever this was about, it had not come for scrap. It had come for something that didn’t know how to hide.
The demand did not come over an open channel.
It arrived the same way the docking request had—quietly, directly, inserted into the station’s internal routing queue with the correct formatting and the correct authority tags.
HARDIN ELLIS VAR — ACTING STEWARD, LOGISTICS NODE K-27
SURRENDER CALEB MOSBY, REGISTERED MASTER OF TRADER VESSEL FARTHER STAR, AND SAEL VARENI IR’ELISAND.
Hardin read it once.
Then again. As if repetition might turn it into a mistake.
There was no threat appended. No countdown. No mention of weapons.
They did not need to say it.
On the wall display, the corvette remained steady beyond the docking arm. Weapon housings open. Power signatures climbing, then settling into a patient, sustainable plateau. Not the flare of intimidation. The comfort of certainty.
Mosby let out a slow breath. “Well,” he said quietly. “That’s personal.”
Sael said nothing. Her eyes moved once across the ops board—traffic drift, hull integrity, Dock Two status, the Farther Star’s outline with her racks still empty and her external pumps exposed. Calculations ran. None ended well. She looked like a woman measuring angles for a fall she already knew was coming.
“They want you,” Hardin said.
“Yeah,” Mosby replied.
Hardin didn’t look at him. He looked at the room. Twenty-seven souls. Salvage bins. Half-eaten trays. The welders who had stopped arguing. The improvised bulkhead that kept the cold out. A whole small civilization balanced on a few pumps and shared stubbornness.
“If I refuse,” Hardin said carefully, “they don’t argue. They demonstrate.”
No one challenged that.
Sael folded her hands loosely behind her back. “They could have boarded already,” she said. “They chose not to.”
“They’re making it clean,” Mosby said. “Clean” was what you called cruelty when it wore gloves.
Hardin’s jaw tightened. This wasn’t some stray patrol. He had let a damaged freighter dock. He had let them weld pumps to the hull. Bolt salvage tanks onto Dock Three. He had let trouble follow them home. He had said yes to people who looked like they needed it. It was an old habit. It had never been safe.
“I brought you in,” Hardin said flatly.
Mosby met his eyes. “You gave us dock. Fuel. Time. That counts.”
“It doesn’t count against a corvette,” Hardin snapped, more sharply than he intended. The anger surprised him—not because it existed, but because it had found a target it could reach.
Silence stretched.
The corvette’s hull swallowed the station lights without reflection.
Hardin exhaled slowly, the anger draining into something steadier. “You don’t step in front of this for me,” he said. “You walk because you choose to.” He made himself say it like a rule, because rules were easier to survive than guilt.
Mosby nodded once. “I choose to.” He said it the way men say yes when they’ve already weighed the no and found it heavier.
Hardin’s gaze hardened. “You’ll walk from here to Dock Two. They’ve agreed.”
Mosby’s brow lifted slightly. “They did?”
The ops board blinked again.
DOCK TWO AUTHORIZATION CONFIRMED.
MOSBY AND SAEL — PROCEED ON FOOT.
NO ESCORT.
The formatting matched the first message. Integrated. Internal. As if it had always been part of the system. Like the station had been built, from the beginning, for their convenience.
Sael’s gaze flicked once toward the Star’s schematic—fuel racks empty, tank cradles half-welded into place, lines hanging open like veins waiting to be connected. Her eyes lingered there a beat too long, and Hardin thought: she’s already counting what she’ll have to steal from fate.
“They’re confident,” she said.
“They should be,” Hardin replied.
Ted spoke from across the room where he sat on an overturned crate, one sleeve rolled back from fresh sealant lines and still favoring his left side when he shifted. “They are correct in their assessment,” he said calmly. “We cannot outrun them. And the station cannot withstand a demonstration.” He sounded almost conversational, which made it worse.
Hardin looked at him for a long moment. “I don’t want to hand you over,” he said. It came out rougher than he intended. Not because Ted needed saving—Ted was beyond pity—but because Hardin didn’t like being told who belonged to whom.
Mosby gave him a thin half-smile. “You’re not. We’re walking.”
It was a small distinction.
It mattered. It was the difference between surrender and choice, and men had died for less.
Behind them, Rheva had gone very still.
She wasn’t looking at the corvette. She was looking at the station schematic—Dock Two glowing amber, pressure doors marked in pale blue, clamp control nodes flagged in tired red. Her stillness wasn’t fear. It was attention sharpening into intent.
She stepped closer to Ted.
“How much authority do they have inside our system?” she asked quietly.
“Enough,” Ted replied. “But not all.”
Hardin shot her a look.
She didn’t return it.
“If a docking arm were to experience mechanical latency,” she continued mildly, “would that be immediately traceable?”
Ted’s head tilted slightly—not confusion, not surprise. Recognition. “Not if the fault originated in aging hydraulic relays,” he said. “Dock Two possesses several that predate the company withdrawal.” His tone was careful, almost polite, as if discussing weather.
Hardin understood then. Not the details. The direction. A plan, assembling itself out of old parts—like everything else they did here.
“This is not a war,” he said sharply.
“No,” Rheva agreed. “It is leverage.” And leverage, Hardin thought, was how the small survived the large. You didn’t win. You shifted weight until something expensive bent.
Her gaze shifted to the bulkhead control cluster.
“Dock Two is the farthest functioning berth,” she said. “Between here and there are six pressure doors. Four still respond to remote override.” She spoke with the mildness of a woman describing a hallway, but her eyes were already walking it.
Ted rose carefully this time, not fragile but aware of the stiffness in his side. He moved closer to the display. His eyes unfocused briefly—not from injury, but from that quiet Talari exchange that did not require raised voices. Data passed in glances. Timing. Load tolerances. Mosby watched them disappear into the ship, then glanced back at the bustle of Dawnreach one last time.
“Alright,” he muttered. “Let’s go home.”
Moments later, the Farther Star eased free of the docking arms, turned her scarred nose toward open space, and began the long burn toward Brightwater.
 
Chapter 27
Brightwater rose from the horizon beneath a pale blue sky, cloud bands drifting lazily over the warm ocean below.
Mosby brought the Farther Star down on manual, easing her through atmosphere with the careful touch of someone who knew every complaint in the hull. She shuddered on entry, drive housings still carrying the scars of hard use, but she held together.
She always did.
The pier stretched out over the water like an old hand.
He settled her onto the landing pads with a soft thud of struts and a sigh of venting air.
For a moment, he simply sat in the cockpit, hands resting on the controls.
Home.
The cargo ramp lowered with its usual tired whine.
Manny was already halfway down the pier, rag in one hand, squinting up at the ship. He stopped, set his hands on his hips, and regarded her in silence.
The mismatched plates. The fresh weld seams. The long scoring scars along her sides. Whole sections where the old paint had burned clean away.
Manny drew a slow breath through his teeth.
“Eh,” he said at last. “She look like she lose a fight.”
Mosby came down the ramp and joined him.
“She bring you back,” Manny added, nodding toward the hull. “That what matter.”
“Yeah,” Mosby said.
Manny stepped closer and ran his hand along a scorched seam.
“You know how much paint this go take?” he asked.
Mosby nodded once. “Yeah.”
Manny glanced at him sideways. “You finally gon’ let me do it proper?”
Mosby’s mouth twitched. “Yeah.”
He turned and walked back up the ramp.
Manny followed.
Inside the cargo bay, Mosby unlatched a stack of sealed containers and cracked one open. Manny leaned in and read the markings.
He blinked.
“Paint.  Heat-rated,” he said. “Vacuum-rated.”
He looked up slowly.
“You serious.”
Mosby gave a small shrug. “We’re not doing patches.”
Manny studied him for a long moment.
Then he nodded.
“Good,” he said simply.
They stood there looking at the stripped metal and scarred plating.
Mosby rested his hand against the bulkhead.
“She’s still got more in her.”
Manny snorted softly.
“So do we.”
He jerked his chin toward the shoreline.
“Come. Let me show you something before you start arguing primer thickness.”
They walked down toward the water, the late sun laying gold across the waves.
Manny pointed beyond the shallows.
“Terraformers finally get the balance right,” he said. “Beach holding better now.”
Mosby followed his gesture.
A small dark head lifted above the water. Then another.
“Turtles,” Manny said. “First nesting tracks last month.”
Mosby watched them move through the surf, slow and deliberate.
“Good,” he said.
They remained there a long while in silence, the ship behind them and the tide moving steadily in.
 
Epilogue
It was late.
No moon. Only a low ceiling of cloud swallowing the stars. Far out over the water, lightning flickered inside a moving storm, silent at this distance, like a thought not yet decided.
Mosby sat in the old chair outside the Farther Star, boots resting on a crate, watching the horizon.
Thinking.
Or perhaps not thinking at all.
He told himself it was the weather he was waiting on. The shift in the air before rain.
He did not look up when he heard footsteps in the sand.
Korrin came down the beach with his trouser legs rolled to his calves, barefoot, shoes hooked through two fingers. He carried two bottles by their necks, condensation beading in the warm night air.
He did not look like a colonel.
He did not look official at all.
He looked like a man who had stepped out of his life for a few hours.
Korrin handed him a bottle without a word and sat on the overturned crate beside him, feet working into the sand.
“I finally got some leave,” Korrin said. “You generated a considerable amount of paperwork for my agency.”
Mosby took a drink. Cold. Bitter. Exactly right.
“That’s what I do.”
Korrin inclined his head slightly, as if that accounted for it.
They watched the storm for a time. Dark waves. Wind shifting. The scent of rain and salt.
At length, Korrin exhaled.
“When Thalean Intelligence traced the Caretaker’s last position,” he said, voice lower now, “they intended to send a retrieval team.”
Mosby did not turn.
“Yes.”
“I suggested an alternative.”
That earned a glance.
Korrin gave a small shrug, still studying the water.
“Sael had already flagged you as a statistical anomaly after Lysara. I ensured her request file reached the top of the stack.”
Mosby’s eyes narrowed. “You pointed her at me.”
“I allowed the trajectory to complete.”
A quiet beat.
“Sael is intelligent. Capable. Ambitious. But she believes in systems. In levers.” His jaw tightened slightly. “I wanted to see what she would do if the lever was a man who does not bend.”
He paused.
“I am not certain I would have done any better in her situation.”
Mosby took another drink.
“And me?”
The corner of Korrin’s mouth moved faintly.
“You,” he said, “have a history of doing the right thing for the wrong reasons. Or the wrong thing for the right ones.”
Korrin stood.
“You do the right thing,” he added. “Last time, I was wrong.”
He started back up the beach and was quickly swallowed by the dark.
Mosby listened to the surf and the low roll of distant thunder.
Then Korrin’s voice carried back, nearly lost in the wind.
“But this time—”
A pause.
“—this time I was right.”
Mosby did not answer.
He watched the storm advance across the water.
