The early rain had given way to what could only be described as a spectacular mid-morning. Warm and humid, as expected for this world, the pier smelled of the sea - salt and algae borne inland by a steady breeze passing over the old concrete baked smooth by decades of sun.  
Caleb Mosby sat in a beach chair beneath the shadow of Farther Star’s nose, the canvas stretched low enough to be comfortable and just inconvenient enough to discourage getting up anytime soon. In one hand he held a fishing pole, the line trailing lazily into the water below. In the other was a bottle of beer that would not stay cold for long, condensation tracing slow paths down the glass.  From a datapad tucked into the pocket of his sun-faded, brightly colored shirt, music drifted out in a tinny, cheerful murmur.
No matter. There were more in the battered cooler beside him, nestled in ice scavenged earlier from the Star’s galley.
The Star was his ship. Supposedly a relic from the last war — surplus — but she was his, battered though she was. An assault shuttle once used to put men and steel into places the other side didn’t want them. Both the forward and aft loading ramps were down, the clamshell doors open wide, letting the salt breeze roll through the cargo bay and out the far end, keeping the interior from turning into an oven beneath the tropical sun.
Brightwater was still being terraformed — nearly finished — but that hadn’t stopped people from settling early. Shallow seas and chains of small islands drew them. Originally planned with leisure in mind, it had been a pleasure world that never quite got its chance. The pier itself had once anchored a massive terraforming processor, long since stripped down and hauled away.
The war had turned the world into a backwater. Who wanted to vacation so close to somewhere another war might break out at any time? The people who came anyway called it home and in time developed their own unhurried way of living. They had taken Mosby in as one of their own, even if he never thought of it in quite those terms.

The song drifted to its natural end, the last notes fading into a soft wash of static. A bright, professional voice slipped in smoothly after it.
“Time for news from around the galaxy. Kestralis officials remain baffled by the failure of the Veylara transit gate.  Khar’Vess Dominion interference has been ruled out. Council Transit Authority investigators report no evidence yet of mechanical fault. Solarian and Astraeum negotiators have reached an impasse over the disputed system from the last war. Minister—”
Mosby reached up and tapped the pad, silencing it.
The quiet rushed back in with the sound of water against pilings and the distant cry of seabirds. He rubbed the scar over his left eye with one finger, slow and absentminded, then let out a long breath that might have been a sigh.
He leaned over, pushed a hand into the cooler’s meltwater, and fished out another bottle. Droplets ran down his wrist as he brought it up. He turned it in his hand, thumb worrying at the label until it peeled away in damp, curling strips.
The slap of wood against metal broke the quiet, followed by the hollow clink of a paint can. Mosby didn’t have to look to know who it was — but he did anyway.
Manny was at it again.
Mosby sighed.
Every so often the old man would appear without warning and begin painting the hull of the Star as if it were the most natural thing in the world. Bright, unapologetic colors had long since claimed the once-drab plating — saints with solemn eyes, icons Mosby half-recognized, symbols and scraps of scripture from a dozen faiths and twice as many stories he couldn’t begin to name. Somewhere along the port side, grinning beneath a careful halo of gold paint, was a portrait Manny called The King.
Elvis, whoever that was.
On cargo runs, the reentry heat and hard vacuum would blister the paint and burn away pieces of it. Manny never complained. He simply came back with fresh cans and added more — new faces, new symbols, new blessings layered over the old.
Early on, Mosby had tried to stop it.
The old man had been halfway through a saint’s face, brush poised delicately at the curve of an eyelid, when Mosby stepped toward the hull with words ready and waiting — practical words about maintenance and resale value and not wanting his ship turned into a traveling shrine.
He never got them out.
A local boy — no more than eight or nine, skinny and sun-baked, all elbows and seriousness — darted in front of him and grabbed his wrist with both hands. The grip was small, but desperate.
“No, Mister Mosby,” the boy said, eyes wide and solemn. “Bad juma if you don’t let him. Ship need blessing. You don’t stop.”
Before Mosby could answer, the boy turned, crossed himself with exaggerated care, and ran back toward town without looking back, as if the matter had already been decided by higher authority.
Mosby stood there a moment, caught between irritation and confusion, the unfinished protest drying up in his throat. By the time he looked up again, Manny hadn’t paused once. The old man was still painting — unbothered, precise — as if nothing at all had happened, as if boys issuing spiritual ultimatums were simply part of the working day.
Mosby let him finish.
Manny himself was thin and wiry, like most people who were born here or had lived long enough to become part of the place. He was perched near the top rung of a ladder leaned against the hull, paint pails hanging from bent-wire hooks, the brush dancing in his hand as though it knew the way without being told. The ladder swayed gently side to side in the rhythm of his work, threatening to tip with every shift of weight, yet somehow never quite doing it.
“What are you painting this time?” Mosby called from his chair, one hand lifted to shade his eyes.
“Eyes,” Manny replied without looking down. “Ship needs eyes. Needs to see, bring you back safe.”
Mosby sighed.
He never quite understood the need to decorate nearly every vehicle on the Brightwater islands in an explosion of color and style. Traffic through town was an endless parade of painted machines — bright panels, hand-drawn symbols, names scrawled in curling scripts. The smaller bikes, with no room for paint, wore tassels and streamers instead, ribbons snapping in the wind like festival banners that had forgotten the festival.
At first, he had tried to persuade Manny not to touch the ship.
He had been politely ignored.
The explanation never changed.
“The ship,” Manny had said once, tapping the hull with a paint-spattered knuckle, “she has a home now. And we want her know she belongs.”
“Most of that’ll burn off next reentry,” Mosby called up now.
Manny smiled at the hull rather than at Mosby, brush still moving in small, careful strokes. “Ship take care of town, take care of people,” he said. “I take care of ship. She good ship. I don’t mind.”
Mosby leaned back in his chair, the fishing line drifting, the beer warming in his hand, and let the breeze pass through the open cargo bay and over him like it had nowhere else it needed to be.
A bottle later, Manny had changed sides and was nearly finished.
The hull looked louder now — the new eyes bright and watchful, lines sharpened, a few final strokes added where Manny felt the ship still needed them. He was rinsing a brush in a dented can when the sound reached them: engines, low and deliberate, tires grinding gravel as they came down the road toward the pier.
Mosby noticed immediately.
Most people didn’t drive this way. They walked the beach instead — shorter, easier, and cooler under bare feet. Vehicles meant purpose. Deliveries. Repairs. Work that couldn’t be carried.
These vehicles meant outsiders.
Manny didn’t look up. He finished the line he was painting, then leaned back slightly to study it.
“People come see you,” he said in the local pidgin, voice casual and unhurried. “They arrive spaceport yesterday. Stay government house last night.”
Mosby frowned slightly and took another drink. His fingers drifted up to the scar over his left eye, rubbing it once without thinking.
He considered who would travel all the way out here to see him — and why Manny, of all people, would know about it.
“What do they want with me?” Mosby asked.
Manny climbed down the ladder, each knee giving a quiet pop as he descended. He sealed the paint cans with practiced care, wiped his hands on a rag that had long ago given up on being clean, then settled a battered hat onto his greying head.
“Not for me say,” he replied.
He looked at Mosby then, just for a moment — and there was something different in his eyes. Not curiosity. Not gossip.
Concern.
He slung the cans into a netted carrier and started up the beach toward his house without another word, bare feet leaving dark prints in the damp sand.
Mosby watched him go.
He had no doubt the old man knew exactly who they were and why they had come. There wasn’t much that happened on the islands that didn’t pass through Manny’s ears sooner or later. The locals treated him with a careful respect, half affection, half superstition — like a man who listened more than he spoke and remembered everything he heard.
Mosby leaned back in his chair, eyes drifting toward the far end of the pier where the sound of the vehicles was growing clearer. He set the empty bottle beside the cooler with the others and reached for a fresh one, holding it in his hand without opening it yet.
The breeze still moved through Farther Star’s open bay.
But the quiet had started to thin.
Two vehicles rolled to a stop where the road ended and gravel gave way to sand. Engines idled for a moment, then died, the sudden absence of noise making the surf seem louder.
Six people got out.
Two were local police — uniforms faded by sun and salt, sleeves rolled, posture relaxed but alert in the way of men who knew the island and the people on it. They stood a little apart from the others, watchful without quite wanting to be involved.
Two more had the look and bearing that announced security before anyone said it out loud: neutral clothing, deliberate movements, eyes that never quite stopped working. The cut of their shirts couldn’t quite hide the bulk of protective vests beneath, and the way their hands rested near their hips told Mosby exactly where the weapons were.
The next person to step from the last vehicle was a woman.
Her dark hair was pulled back with precise care, not a strand out of place despite the humidity. Her clothing matched the security detail in tone but not in purpose — tailored rather than practical, chosen to move well without drawing attention. She paused a moment after closing the door, letting her eyes adjust to the light off the water.
But it was the last man who caught — and held — Mosby’s attention.
Lieutenant Colonel Alastair Korrin.
No uniform. No visible insignia. And yet he stood there for a moment, motionless, as if he were about to dress down some young marine who had failed to salute. Authority clung to him the way humidity clung to the air — invisible, unavoidable.
His gaze swept the scene methodically: Mosby in the chair, the beer bottle in his hand, the fishing pole resting idle. Then his eyes reached the ship.
They stopped there.
A visible grimace crossed Korrin’s face, quick and unguarded, gone almost before it settled.
He turned to one of the security men and took a folder from him, flipping it open without breaking stride. His eyes flicked across the contents. Satisfied, he closed it and tucked it under his arm as he started across the sand toward the pier, walking with the clipped, purposeful pace of a man who expected the ground to cooperate simply because he was using it.
The woman followed a step behind him.
Mosby noticed immediately that she wasn’t bothered by the Star’s appearance. Her eyes moved differently — not reacting, not judging, but taking in details and quietly cataloging them. The hull. The open ramps. The breeze passing clean through the cargo bay. Then him. Then the breakwater. Then the ocean beyond, as if mapping the entire place in a single pass.
She looked human.
But something about her didn’t quite settle.
Before Mosby could put a name to it, they reached the pier and stopped a few meters away.
“Captain Caleb Mosby,” Korrin said. His voice was flat, the words clipped as though he were biting back something sharper.
Mosby didn’t stand.
“Lieutenant Colonel Alastair Korrin,” he replied, equally flat, not bothering to acknowledge the woman at Korrin’s shoulder. He let the title hang there — outdated “Last I heard,” Mosby added mildly, “you were denied promotion.”
Korrin’s jaw tightened. “We are both no longer in the Marines. I have a new position — Sector Subdirector, Solarian Intelligence. I have an assignment for you—”
“No,” Mosby said, not raising his voice.
“I’m not in your unit. I’m not part of your intelligence. You don’t get to order me.”
Korrin’s eyes flicked briefly to the Star, then back.
Mosby turned his head and met his gaze. “I’m out. That’s what discharged means.”
Color crept up Korrin’s neck despite the control he was trying to maintain. His fingers tightened once on the folder under his arm.
Before he could press forward, the woman stepped smoothly into the space between them.
The movement was unhurried, precise — placing her just enough between the two men to change the shape of the moment without openly challenging either of them.
“We’re not asking you to enlist,” she said calmly, a faint, practiced smile touching her lips. “We just need you to fly.”
“It’s never that simple, is it, Colonel,” Mosby said coldly. “It wasn’t then, and it isn’t now. So why me? And why now?”
“We need someone who can fly into places others can’t or won’t,” Korrin said. “And a ship that can get him there.”
His mouth tightened.
“And,” he added, the words edged now with something personal, “because I don’t like you.”
The woman didn’t react to Korrin’s admission. If anything, she looked faintly relieved that it had finally been said out loud.
“That’s not the whole of it,” she said smoothly, before Mosby could answer. “But it’s close enough to be honest.”
Korrin shot her a sharp look.
She ignored it.
She turned her attention fully to Mosby then, posture open without being casual, hands folded loosely in front of her as if this were a conversation she had been expecting to have all along.
“You have a ship that can enter contested space without announcing itself,” she continued. “You have clearance histories that still resolve in the gate system, even if they shouldn’t. And you have experience flying without support, without oversight, and without the expectation that anyone is coming to help you if things go wrong.”
Mosby listened without comment.
“And,” she added, “you are here.”
Korrin folded his arms. “Don’t flatter him.”
“I’m not,” she said, still looking at Mosby. “I’m explaining why this didn’t go to a committee.”
She took a step closer, close enough now that Mosby could see the faint lines at the corners of her eyes — not age, exactly, but use. The look of someone who had watched people make decisions that mattered.
“We’re not asking you to rejoin anything,” she said again. “No uniform. No command structure. No flag on your hull.”
Korrin exhaled through his nose. “For now.”
She didn’t contradict him.
“What we’re asking,” she went on, “is that you take the Farther Star somewhere we cannot officially go, and come back with what ever is found.”
Mosby’s gaze drifted past her — to the ship, to the paint drying on the hull, to the open ramps and the breeze moving through the bay like it always had.
“And if I say no?” he asked.
“You don’t,” Korrin snapped. The restraint finally broke — or appeared to. “You can be recalled to service and ordered.”
Mosby didn’t move.
“And your ship,” Korrin went on, the words sharpening into something personal, “how you got it, why it was improperly released as surplus — all of that can be reviewed.” He sneered, gesturing toward the riot of color on the hull. “Government property reclaimed. Frankly, it’d be cheaper to scrap it than try to strip all that off.”
“You try that,” Mosby said calmly, “and half your paperwork eats the other half. The rest gets buried so deep no one admits it ever existed.”
For a moment, neither of them spoke.
Korrin’s expression shifted — not rage, not surprise. Recognition. The look of a man reminded that some things had never gone the way he had planned.
“You always did think you knew better,” Korrin quietly growled.
Mosby met his eyes. “I did. At least once.”
The woman stepped forward before Korrin could push it further, her movement smooth, unhurried. She gave Korrin a look — measured, warning, final.
He stopped.
“Things don’t have to be adversarial,” she said evenly. “If you say no, you remain free.”
She let that settle.
“It will simply take more effort to stay that way. Licenses take longer. Fuel costs more. Gate access stops resolving cleanly. Nothing dramatic. Nothing you could point to.”
Her eyes stayed on Mosby’s. Korrin said nothing.
Mosby smiled faintly. “That’s what you call not ordering me around.”
She inclined her head, acknowledging the point. “It’s what I call telling you the shape of the world you’re already in.”
Mosby studied her for a long moment.
“You are worse than that bastard.”
There was a twitch at the corner of her mouth when he said it.
The words weren’t loud. There was no heat in them. Just fatigue, and something old that had finally been allowed to surface.
For the first time since she’d stepped onto the pier, she didn’t answer immediately. She turned her gaze to the ocean and let it rest there — long enough to feel deliberate, not evasive.
“Can I have a moment alone with Mr. Mosby,” she said at last, holding her hand out without looking at Korrin.
The request was polite. The gesture wasn’t.
Korrin hesitated, the folder still in his hand. For a moment it looked like he might argue — might try to reclaim ground he’d already ceded. Then his jaw tightened and he handed it to her instead.
She took it, didn’t open it, and tucked it under her arm.
“Wait by the vehicles,” she added.
Korrin turned away without a word. The security men followed, boots crunching softly as they moved back toward the road. The local police drifted off as well, leaving the pier to the sound of water and wind again.
Only then did Mosby really look at her.
She was tall, though not in a way that demanded notice. Long lines, balanced proportions — everything about her arranged for efficiency rather than display. She stood easily in the heat, weight centered, posture relaxed without ever becoming loose. Not Solarian. Too still. Too composed. Something quieter. More ingrained.
Her features were fine-boned and even, skin smooth and unmarked by sun or scar. The humidity hadn’t touched her the way it touched everyone else on the pier. Her hair was pulled back simply, dark and glossy, held in place without ornament. No jewelry. No visible tech. Nothing unnecessary.
Her eyes met his.
They were dark and steady, attentive without pressing. She didn’t stare. She observed, letting the moment come to her. When she looked at Mosby, it wasn’t curiosity or judgment.
It was assessment — complete and unhurried.
She looked human.
She just didn’t look Solarian.
“But he’s not wrong about one thing,” she said. “This situation exists whether you participate or not. We need a man of your talents.”
Mosby nodded once. “Who are you?”
She opened the folder and slid a single sheet free. Not credentials. Not orders. A travel authorization, already populated. She set it on the crate between them and turned it so he could read it.
“My name is Sael, Thalean Ministry of Security,” she said. “And I’ll be going with you.”
“That wasn’t the question,” Mosby said.
“It’s the part that matters,” Sael replied. “Names are only useful when someone has to share space with you.”
Mosby glanced at the page, then back at her. “You don’t look like cargo, and you’re a long way from Thalean space.”
“I’m not cargo,” she said. “I’m there to make sure this doesn’t become something it shouldn’t.”
Mosby rose from the chair and reached for the folder. “Let me see what this is all about.”
She didn’t hand it to him.
She watched him for a moment, longer than was comfortable, measuring something that had nothing to do with the paper. Then she closed the folder and tucked it back under her arm.
For a heartbeat, that seemed final.
“All right,” she said.
She handed it to him.
“I’ll be back this evening,” she added. “After you’ve looked things over and thought about it.”
She turned and walked back toward the vehicles without waiting for a reply. Korrin was already there, rigid beside the transport, eyes fixed anywhere but the pier. Doors closed. Engines started. Gravel crunched as the vehicles rolled away and disappeared beyond the rise.
Mosby stood on the pier with the folder in his hand, the Star breathing behind him, the paint on her hull still drying in the sun.
A bit later, Manny came back.
He went straight to the ladder where it already leaned against the Star’s forward flank and climbed the rungs, stopping to look over the hull. He tilted his head slightly, not searching, just taking it in.
Mosby watched from the chair, the folder still unopened on his knee.
Manny rested his hand on the metal near one of the newly painted eyes. The paint was still tacky. He left his hand there a moment longer than necessary. His lips moved, the sound too soft to make out — words without shape, carried more by habit than intent.
He drew his hand back.
“They coming back,” Manny said.
“Yes,” Mosby said.
Manny nodded once. “You going with them?”
Mosby glanced at the folder, then back at the ship. “I don’t know yet. Don’t know I have a choice.”
Manny accepted that without comment.
“She got eyes now. That helps,” he said finally, as he gathered his things and started back toward town, unhurried.
Mosby didn’t open the folder right away.
As the afternoon wore on, Mosby read the dossier in the folder.
Then he read it again.
It was thin. Almost insultingly so.
Fly to the Ael-Runn system.
Wait.
Then return.
No contingencies. No timelines beyond soon. No explanation of what they were supposed to be waiting for.
The problem wasn’t the instructions.
The problem was Ael-Runn.
He leaned back in the chair, folder resting on his chest, eyes drifting to the bright line where sky met sea.
Ael-Runn sat inside the Astraeum Protectorate.
Four years ago, the Protectorate had been at war, firing on ships that flew under the same flags Mosby used to wear.
Officially neutral now. Publicly reconciled. Diplomats smiling for cameras.
But space didn’t forget as quickly as press releases did.
A patrol boat had once held Mosby’s transport group in a targeting grid for seven full minutes after the ceasefire, “confirming transponder validity.” Nobody fired. Nobody spoke. They all just waited, knowing exactly how little it would take to start things again.
That was the sort of place Ael-Runn was.
Most Astraeum citizens weren’t fanatics — not loud ones, anyway. They didn’t rant.
They believed.
Deeply. Quietly. Structurally.
The Luminoti wasn’t decoration to them. It wasn’t something you practiced on weekends.
It was how law was justified. How strangers were judged. How wars were explained afterward.
Mosby scratched at the scar over his eye.
That alone would have made the job delicate.
Doing it in a Solarian-built ship, with a Thalean operative on board, in a system that had buried people because of both of those governments…
That made it something else entirely.
He looked down at the folder again.
Wait, it said.
In Astraeum space.
Uninvited.
He closed it.
As he considered his options, his stomach reminded him he hadn’t eaten yet that day. Judging by the angle of the sun lowering toward the water, he could expect company soon, too.
With a sigh, he set the folder aside and pulled out a small grill from the cargo bay. After a moment’s hesitation, he added a narrow table and two chairs.
If they were going to ask him to do something stupid, they could at least do it over decent food.
As the sun touched the ocean and the local prawns neared the end of their time on the grill, Mosby heard footsteps on the rear ramp.
He didn’t turn.
Sael came up into the cargo bay with an easy, unhurried grace, as if the ship had already agreed to her presence. She paused just inside, her attention drawn to the built-in equipment racks along the bulkhead — scarred mounts, lock points polished by use, the quiet geometry of things meant to survive hard landings.
Then she continued forward, passing through the bay and out toward the open ramp that faced the pier.
“I didn’t hear you drive up,” Mosby observed.
“I walked down the beach,” she said. “I don’t get to enjoy the sights often.”
She had changed. The field jacket was gone. In its place was a simple local dress — light fabric, muted color, cut for the heat. Her hair was loose now, falling around her shoulders instead of pulled back with military neatness.
“That smells good,” she added. “I didn’t know you could cook.”
Mosby flipped one of the prawns, watching the shell darken and split over the coals.
“Ship rations get old after a while,” he said. “So, I learned to cook whatever the local economy has to offer.” He turned the next one with practiced care. “Folks in town can’t always pay in credits. I take trade — whatever they can spare.”
“They rely on you, then.”
“Not many traders come this way,” Mosby replied. “I run a regular circuit every month — Aureline, Kallamar, Paralax, a few nearby systems. I was due to bring back microbiome stabilizers and some other supplies for the hospital on this run.”
He began transferring the prawns to a plate. Steam curled upward into the cooling evening air.
“It doesn’t make much more than fuel and maintenance,” he added, “but I don’t need much more. It helps people out. Medicine. Mail. Things we don’t have locally.”
Sael studied him for a moment. Not his face — his hands, the careful economy of his movements, the way the ship stood open behind him like part of the conversation.
“Mind if I sit?” she asked.
“Go ahead.”
She took the chair across from him. As he set the plate down and reached for the wine, Mosby caught the faintest trace of something unfamiliar when she shifted in the breeze.
Not perfume, exactly. Something warmer. Cinnamon, maybe. Cloves. Sweet, sharp, and out of place against salt air and grilled shellfish.
He didn’t comment on it.
But he noticed.
“You’ve had time to read the dossier,” she said as he poured the wine and handed her a glass.
“I did.”
Mosby sat and served a portion onto each plate. The prawns steamed faintly between them. He didn’t rush to eat.
“There wasn’t much to it,” he went on. “We leave here, head to the Ael-Runn system, land, wait while you do whatever it is you’re there to do, then come back.” He glanced at her over the rim of his glass. “If that’s really all there is, you could’ve booked passage on a commercial liner. Would’ve been cheaper. Quieter, too.”
Sael took a sip of wine and held the glass for a moment before setting it down. Her movements were unhurried, precise. Composed.
“You know it isn’t that simple,” she said.
Mosby gave a short, humorless snort. “Yeah. I do.” He leaned back slightly. “And it’s going to be rougher than you’re letting on.”
She didn’t interrupt.
“Ael-Runn sits inside the Protectorate,” he continued. “They’re touchy about who comes into their space on a good day. After the war?” He shook his head. “They don’t see outsiders as visitors. They see them as problems.”
He finally picked up his fork, more out of habit than appetite.
“And that’s before you factor in the Luminoti.”
Sael’s expression didn’t change — but something behind her eyes did.
“Of course,” she said. “That’s why you’re flying me in.”
She took a bite, then paused. Her expression shifted — not surprise, but genuine pleasure.
“Oh,” she said quietly. “This is good.”
She ate another bite before continuing, as if the food had earned her patience.
“You flew into worse places during the war,” she went on. “I read your file. Even the classified parts.” Her gaze held his. Steady. Present. “At least no one is shooting at you this time.”
Mosby set his fork down.
The sound was soft. Deliberate.
“We won’t have a team of operators backing us up either,” he said. “No overwatch. No extraction window. Just you, me, and the Star.”
Sael didn’t argue.
“I can fly her,” Mosby continued. “I can keep us out of trouble most days. But if this goes sideways — and it will — I’m going to need someone who can keep the ship working while I’m busy doing something else.”
He watched her carefully now.
“Engineering,” he said. “And it wouldn’t hurt if they knew how to move in places where questions come with consequences.”
Sael took another bite, slower this time. Thoughtful.
“You already have someone in mind,” she said.
Mosby picked up his glass, took a sip, then nodded once. “An old contact.”
She didn’t ask more.
“Two hundred thousand credits,” Mosby said after a moment. “Half up front. And another hundred thousand to cover the engineer.”
Sael didn’t blink.
She set her glass down and extended her hand across the table.
“Agreed.”
The word came too quickly.
Mosby didn’t take her hand.
“You didn’t even ask why.”
“I don’t need to,” she replied. “That number tells me how dangerous you think this is. And how much you’re willing to tolerate being lied to.”
A faint breath left him. Not quite a laugh.
“You’re not even pretending this is a negotiation.”
She met his eyes. Calm. Certain. “You weren’t pretending either.”
For a moment they sat there, the sound of the tide moving beneath the pier filling the space between them. Behind them, the Star’s cooling systems ticked softly as metal adjusted to the night air.
Finally, Mosby took her hand.
Her grip was firm. Not dominant. Not tentative. Just sure.
The evening settled in around them, warm and salt-heavy, while the ship loomed quiet and watchful behind her — eyes freshly painted, staring out toward a future neither of them fully trusted.
The next two days started early.
When the Relay office opened, Mosby was already waiting outside with a cup of bitter station coffee and a list of names he hadn’t had to use in a while. He sent messages to traders he knew and trusted — people who understood quiet work and didn’t ask more questions than necessary. He estimated it would take at least a day before any replies came back.
After that he arranged for cargo to be ready for loading in two days’ time, then returned to the Star and began moving through her methodically, securing anything loose that might rattle free under hard thrust.
The afternoon and the next day were spent checking and double-checking every system: power relays, cooling loops, hull seams along the forward flanks where Black Irons always took stress on violent entries. He worked with a light clamped between his teeth more often than not, collecting minor cuts on each hand and grease up to his elbows as he pulled open maintenance hatches that hadn’t been touched in months.
The ship smelled different when she was opened up like this — metal, insulation, the faint sharp tang of coolant.
Sael appeared at some point without announcement.
He found her in the forward systems bay, sleeves rolled, diagnostic slate open, quietly recalibrating a sensor cluster that had been drifting off true for longer than he liked to admit.
“You don’t have to do that,” he said, leaning against the hatch frame.
“It was wrong,” she replied without looking up. “If I’m risking my life in this ship, I want it right.”
There was no accusation in it. Just fact.
He watched a moment longer, then nodded once and moved on.
The next morning, when the haulers arrived with the cargo, she took charge of the loading without asking permission.
Most of it was seafood packed in insulated crates and a small amount of local produce bound for markets farther along Mosby’s usual route. The beach road filled with the smell of brine and engine exhaust as the haulers idled with their ramps down.
Sael moved through the cargo bay with the easy competence of someone who understood weight before theory. She balanced loads across the deck without being told how, keeping heavier crates low and forward, leaving the centerline clear. Nothing went near the access hatches. Nothing where it might shift under lateral thrust.
When she noticed Mosby watching, she said simply, “I worked logistics. Started as a loader. Moved up from there before getting into government work.”
Mosby shrugged. That tracked. He let her continue.
By noon most of the cargo had been secured, netting cinched tight, tie-downs checked twice. The last hauler rolled away down the beach road, leaving tire tracks that would be gone by morning tide.
Manny came walking along the sand as if this were any other day.
Trousers rolled. Hat in hand. The slow, patient stride of a man who had never once hurried for someone else’s schedule.
He carried a small envelope pouch tucked under his arm. Inside were printed relay texts — the replies to the messages Mosby had sent the morning before. Short, practical ones. A woman on Aureline who owed him a favor. A retired courier on Kallamar who still liked to feel useful. Each had agreed to cover the route Mosby normally ran until he returned.
After reading them, Mosby handed the messages back to Manny.
“Things’ll be fine till I get back,” he said. “Jacobson might overcharge a bit, but I can cover that later.”
“Town good to know,” Manny said, eyes moving over the ship, taking in the change in her usual relaxed, open feel. The ramps were still down, but the air around her had shifted. Less harbor. More departure.
Then Manny brought out what he’d been carrying in his other hand.
A necklace of bright braided cord, strung with small shells and a carved turtle of dark driftwood, worn smooth from handling.
Without ceremony, Manny slipped it over Mosby’s head and let it settle against his chest.
“What’s this for?” Mosby asked, lifting the turtle between thumb and forefinger.
Manny shrugged. “For the journey. Turtle knows. Comes back to same shore.” He nodded once toward the ship. “Figure you should too.”
He didn’t wait for thanks. He simply turned and started back toward town.
“Hey,” Mosby called after him, “how do you know about turtles? Terraformers haven’t brought any in yet!”
Manny raised a hand without looking back.
“Don’t need to see them,” he called. “I know.”
Sael was tightening the last cargo strap when Mosby came up the aft ramp, boots ringing softly on the metal decking.
“We good to go?” he asked.
“Last one,” she said, giving the strap a final pull. “Doesn’t shift under lateral thrust now.”
“Good.” He glanced toward the stack of cases near the bulkhead. “Got all your gear?”
She pointed. “Everything I need.”
“Fine. You’ve got the copilot’s bunk. Stow your gear there. Any firearms go in the weapons locker,” Mosby said, reaching past her to thumb the control panel. The aft ramp began to rise, hydraulics whining as the clamshell doors folded inward and sealed with a heavy, final thud.
“Why is that?” she asked.
“My ship, my rules.” He gave her a level look. “I don’t want anything loose that could be trouble once we’re underway. You don’t like it, we can call this off right now.”
Sael held his gaze a moment longer than necessary. Then she nodded once. “Fine. But I want the access code.”
“I figured as much,” Mosby said, already turning away toward the forward passage.
Twenty minutes later, Mosby sat in the cockpit, running through preflight. The boards glowed in layered colors, status bars creeping steadily toward green as systems came online. He moved through the checklist by habit, fingers flipping guarded switches, eyes skimming readouts that had become as familiar as his own hands.
Sael entered quietly and slid into the copilot’s seat, fastening her harness with efficient, practiced motions.
“Are we ready?” she asked.
“Just about,” Mosby said. He keyed the transmitter.
“Brightwater Port Control, trader vessel Farther Star, requesting flight plan activation.”
A moment passed. Then an out-of-breath voice came back, slightly distorted by the old relay equipment. “Farther Star, Brightwater Control, go ahead.”
Mosby smiled faintly and continued working. The ship’s systems deepened in tone as the reactors shifted from standby to flight readiness. A low vibration built through the deck — not loud, but full of restrained promise, like a big animal thinking about running.
“Filed outbound to Aureline via direct burn,” he said. “Two souls aboard, cargo secured. Request plan activation and departure clearance.”
Sael raised an eyebrow. “Being a bit officious, aren’t you?”
“They don’t get much traffic,” Mosby said. “Makes ’em feel important. Keeps me in practice too.”
The voice came back. “Plan activated at 13:12 local. No other departures scheduled. You’re clear whenever ready, Mosby.”
“Brightwater Traffic, Farther Star lifting in two minutes.”
“Copy that. Surface corridor clear. Winds light out of the west. You mind if my cousin uses your pier while you’re gone?”
Mosby chuckled. “Sure. Tell him just don’t scuff anything.”
“No promises,” the voice replied.
Mosby eased the throttles forward.
The Farther Star lifted fast and clean, rising off the pier in a column of shimmering heat. The water below flattened under the thrust wash, ripples racing outward from the pilings as the ship clawed for altitude. The pier, the town, and the bright scatter of painted rooftops dropped away beneath them.
Blue faded to indigo, then to deepening violet. The horizon curved, subtle at first, then undeniable. Clouds thinned below like torn gauze, their tops catching sunlight that no longer reached the ground.
The vibration shifted as the atmosphere thickened against the hull, then began to fall away. A shudder ran through the frame as they punched through a band of rough air.
Sael’s fingers tightened around the armrests.
“You all right?” Mosby asked, not looking away from the instruments.
She nodded once, too quickly. “Not used to this much turbulence.”
Mosby’s mouth twitched. “Lady, you ain’t seen nothing yet.”
Her head snapped toward him, eyes widening just a fraction.
His grin deepened, but his hands never stopped moving.
“Brightwater Traffic, Farther Star passing six-zero thousand meters, transitioning to orbital climb,” he transmitted.
A moment later the reply crackled through. “Logged, Farther Star. Safe flight, Mosby. Relay office will forward any messages.”
The sky darkened toward black ahead of them, and the first hard stars began to burn through.
Behind them, Brightwater turned slowly beneath a wash of afternoon light — already looking farther away than Mosby liked to admit.

Clearing the planet’s gravity well took longer than most people expected, and Mosby wasn’t pushing the engines hard. Their power came as a steady vibration through the ship — a low tremor in the deck and up through the seat frame. The climb was deliberate, efficient, unhurried, wasting no fuel as the Farther Star curved away and Brightwater fell behind.
Sael said little during ascent. Her hands stayed on the armrests, though the death grip she’d started with had eased. As violet faded to black and the world slipped out of view, she settled back and took several slow breaths.
“You okay?” Mosby asked, adjusting course, his hands moving easily across the controls.
“I…” She grimaced. “That felt rougher than I’m used to.”
“You’re used to civilian craft. They run heavier stabilizers and dampeners than what the Star was built with,” he said. “I should apologize, though. Assault pilots turn the dampeners down to get a better feel for the ship going in on a run. Rides rougher, but that’s what I’m used to.” He glanced over, sincere. “I’ll reset them to full next time.”
Sael closed her eyes and let out a slow breath. “No… I’ll manage. You do what you need to get us there.”
Two hours later Mosby began the FTL checklist, eyes moving across displays and instruments as his hands worked the controls by habit. Sael had relaxed once they were clear of the planet. She returned from the galley with two mugs and offered one across the console.
“Coffee?”
“Oh, no thanks,” Mosby said. “Four and a half years in the Marines and I never could get used to the nasty stuff.”
Sael settled into the copilot’s seat, cupping the mug in both hands. “It doesn’t grow on Astrin, and we couldn’t afford it anyway.” She took a sip, closing her eyes briefly as she savored it. “I learned to like it working logistics on a station in Orenai.”
“Orenai?” Mosby glanced at her. “That’s Solarian.”
Sael lowered the mug to her lap, still holding it. A faint crease formed between her brows. “I’m the fourth daughter of a minor house. There wasn’t much prospect for me, so I took a government post. They assigned me to Orenai as a logistics liaison.”
“So you were a Lady stacking boxes,” Mosby observed, continuing down the checklist.
“Yes,” she said, straightening in her seat, chin lifting a fraction. “But it got me to where I am now — and where I will be soon.”
Mosby paused at that, one hand resting lightly on the console. Then, as if nothing of weight had just been spoken, he said, “Well, do you mind checking the cargo before we go to FTL?”
Sael studied him for a moment, then her expression softened. “Certainly.” She set the mug into its holder, unstrapped, and moved aft.
Mosby watched her go, gave a small shake of his head, and turned back to the instruments.
“All good?” he asked when she returned.
“Everything secure,” she said, settling into her seat. “But the ship’s going to smell like fish soon.”
He sighed. “Can’t be helped. Not much else comes off Brightwater until the harvest comes in. I’ll get new filters at Aureline Transit, but it’ll linger for a month.” He tapped the final confirmation. “All right — let’s go to FTL.”
Sael tightened her straps.
“Don’t overdo it,” Mosby said. “This won’t be like leaving atmo. This’ll be… different. Smooth, but different.”
She looked relieved. “I’ve been through jumps before, but always in liners or cargo haulers. I’m a rated pilot, you know. Those ships flew almost entirely by instruments.” She gestured toward the forward ports. “You couldn’t see a thing outside.”
Mosby leaned back slightly. “Okay… here we go.”
The FTL drive began to spool up, and the ship filled with a low, steady hum.
The world outside changed.
FTL wasn’t a flash or a streak of stars. Instead, space ahead softened, like heat haze over a desert road. A faint shimmer gathered before the bow, then spread, blooming outward until it swallowed the view and even the Farther Star seemed dim beside it.
Outside, the universe stretched.
Stars did not streak — they bent, smeared into impossible geometries, long curved distortions like light seen through rippling water. Shapes lingered at the edge of perception: threads, arcs, deep wells of shadow that seemed to slide past without ever quite moving.
Inside, a low harmonic vibration filled the ship — not loud, but present everywhere at once. Now and then came the subtle sensation of turning, though no thrusters fired and no course change showed on the board.
Sael exhaled slowly. “It looks different than I expected. It feels like we’re inside something.”
“We are,” Mosby replied. “Just don’t ask me to explain what.”
After that there was little to do once the navigation computer took over. Mosby stayed near the cockpit, dozing in short stretches, waking often to scan the instruments or when a subtle shift in the ship’s motion stirred him.
When the second watch cycle would normally begin, Sael appeared with food and two cups.
“You sleep like a man expecting problems,” she said, setting the tray down.
Mosby’s eyes opened partway. “I sleep like a man who’s had problems,” he said. “Another old habit from flying alone so long. There isn’t anyone else.” He pushed himself upright and began eating. “Pretty good,” he added.
“Thanks.” She checked the instruments. “It isn’t good for you. You should get proper sleep. I can handle things, and you won’t be any use worn out if something happens.”
“Alright,” Mosby said, standing and stretching. “Let’s see what you can do.”
Sael slid into the seat. He stood behind her, watching — smooth inputs, no overcorrection, her eyes traveling steadily across the board.
“What do you think?” he asked.
“Transit’s faster than a hauler, but nothing I can’t handle. Not much to do, really.”
“Wake me if drift exceeds point-zero-three.”
“It won’t.”
“That wasn’t a suggestion,” he growled.
She waited until he’d gone. “I know,” she said softly, smiling.
The next day passed in the quiet rhythm of ship life. One of them always in or near the cockpit. Meals shared, watches rotated, systems checked and rechecked. As Sael had said, there wasn’t much to do.
On the third day, a chime sounded.
“FTL exit approach in twenty,” Sael said over the intercom.
“I’ll be there in a minute,” came Mosby’s reply.
He entered the cockpit, stretching. “How are things?”
“Everything within parameters,” she said, starting to rise.
Mosby put a hand on her shoulder. “You ever piloted an exit?”
“Once. It was a while back. Mostly automated.”
“Well this is a quiet system. No better time to do it manually.” He slid into the copilot’s seat.
Sael took a breath and began the transition. Slowly, space outside clarified. The stretched impressions of stars tightened back into points. A pale system primary bloomed ahead.
“Easy,” Mosby murmured. “Don’t rush the transition.”
“I’m not. Sensors show no obstructions at exit point.”
The field peeled away like water shedding from a hull. Normal space settled around them with a subtle shudder.
Sael let out a breath.
“Welcome back to the slow universe,” Mosby said.
Traffic lanes resolved. Civilian transponders pinged lazily — haulers, couriers, a bulk transport crawling in-system.
Routine. Orderly.
Mosby keyed the comm.
“Aureline Traffic, independent trader Farther Star inbound from outer vector. Request approach corridor and docking assignment.”


“Farther Star, wait one at the outer marker.”
“Mosby,” Sael said, a note of concern in her voice, “transient return on the scope.”
“Where?” he said, leaning to get a better look.
She marked the vector, but it now showed empty space. “Sensor echo from the transition?” she asked, not sounding certain.
Mosby didn’t answer.
A second later the contact returned—closer this time, still outside visual range, profile too thin for a freighter, too steady for debris.
And matching their vector.
Mosby’s jaw tightened. “That’s not traffic.”
The contact shifted again, sliding just far enough off their sensor edge to make a clean lock impossible.
Sael’s voice stayed level, but her hands were very still on the controls. “It’s pacing us.”
“Yeah,” Mosby said quietly. “It is.”
He checked the sensors and the comm. No registry string. No trade ID. Just a clean, blank return where data ought to have been. He stiffened.
Sael noticed the change in his posture. “Problem?”
“Maybe nothing,” he said. “Got a ghost out there.”
He magnified the contact. The return was thin, almost too neat, like a shape cut out of space rather than built in it.
“Military?” she asked.
“Not broadcasting like one,” Mosby said. “Not broadcasting like anything.”
He keyed a polite, low-power ping on standard trader frequencies. “Unidentified traffic, this is independent trader Farther Star inbound from Brightwater. You showing passive only, or you got something to say?”
No response.
The contact held its course for several minutes more, then made a shallow burn adjustment. Not toward them. Not away. Just… sideways. As if stepping out of a line only it could see.
A minute later it disappeared from the scanner display, gone as cleanly as it had appeared.
Sael kept her eyes on the screen even after the marker vanished. “Could be a survey craft,” she said. “Or a courier running dark.”
“Could be,” Mosby agreed.
He didn’t add that survey craft filed notices, and couriers running dark didn’t drift that close to a known trade lane without saying hello.
“What do you think?” Sael asked, more quietly now.
“Don’t know,” Mosby said. “I’m just a pilot. This is more your world than mine.”
She watched him for a moment, calculating, lips pressed tight, then looked back at the scanner. “It’s probably nothing to worry about.”
Then the comm came to life. “Farther Star, Aureline Control. You’re cleared Dock Ring C, Berth Twelve. Vector two-seven-eight mark zero-zero-three. Watch your drift—we’ve got a tanker cycling thrusters on the inner lane.”
“Copy that,” Mosby said, switching seats with Sael.
He made the course corrections smoothly and guided the Star through the traffic lanes. Small adjustments brought them into alignment with the berth. A soft shudder ran through the hull as the docking clamps took hold. Mosby powered down the engines and let the station’s handling system ease the Star the rest of the way in.
Sael watched as he completed the docking sequence. “You make that look easy.”
“It is,” he said. “Right up until it isn’t.”
The smell reached the dockworkers before the ramp had finished lowering.
One of them wrinkled his nose, then grinned. “Brightwater catch again?”
“Fresh this time,” Mosby called down.
The air in the cargo bay was still cool from transit, but the sharp brine of packed fish cut through insulation and cold metal alike. The workers came aboard with practiced efficiency, boots ringing on the deck plates as they rolled in pallet jacks and shoulder braces. Crates of insulated seafood went first, their lids beaded with condensation. Then came the smaller bins—leafy island greens wrapped in damp cloth, knobby root vegetables still carrying a trace of red soil in their creases.
Payment transferred in small, practical numbers. No ceremony. No bargaining theater. Just figures reviewed on a handheld slate, a nod, and a confirmation tap. Not enough to make anyone rich, but enough to keep routes alive and names remembered.
Sael handled the manifests without being asked. She stood just off the main flow of traffic, slate in hand, cross-checking weights against declared loads and verifying signatures with quick, precise motions. Her expression stayed neutral, but she worked with the quiet intensity of someone who did not like leaving loose ends behind.
Across the bay, Mosby leaned against a bulkhead, speaking with the dock receiver while two workers pried open the lid of a seafood crate.
A breath of cold brine rolled out as the seal cracked.
The receiver prodded one of the inner packs with a gloved finger, then frowned. “Ice is softer than it should be.”
“It always is by the time it gets here,” Mosby said. “Your loading bay sat open half the transfer window.”
The man pressed a thumb against a gel pack, watching it deform. “Might have to mark this as partial temperature compromise.”
“Might,” Mosby agreed. “If you want the paperwork headache. Or you can call it within tolerance and move it while it’s still good.”
The receiver glanced at him sideways. He knew the dance as well as Mosby did. Mark it spoiled and the forms multiplied. Mark it usable and everyone kept their day simple.
“How far over do you think you are?” the man asked.
“Less than two percent,” Mosby said. “Same as every other time your dock runs behind schedule.”
A pause.
The receiver grunted. “Call it acceptable loss.”
“Thought you might.”
By the time the last Brightwater crate rolled down the ramp, the cargo bay was already filling again. Machine parts in reinforced cases. Medical supplies with tamper seals and temperature tags blinking steady green. A row of sealed containers marked for Karamar and further along the line, their destination codes stenciled in faded blue.
Routine. Ordinary. Safe.
Which made Mosby more uneasy than he liked.
He stood at the top of the ramp a moment longer than necessary, watching the dock beyond the workers’ backs. Cranes moved. Cargo floated between guide beams. Station traffic drifted in its slow, deliberate patterns.
Nothing there.
He rubbed the scar over his eye with his thumb. The shadow ship lingered in his mind, wrong in a way he couldn’t name.
He had seen it only once, out in open space—black against black, a hull line that didn’t reflect starlight the way it should have. Just a shape where no shape ought to be, sliding across his sensors and then gone.
Nothing he could point to. Nothing he could prove.
And that was what he didn’t like.
Behind him, paperwork finished cycling through the dock net, confirmations chiming softly from the cargo office console. Sael stepped up beside him.
“Want to get something to eat that isn’t ship rations?” Mosby asked.
“Know any place that’s good?”
“Maybe. I want to check for messages on the way.”
Inside the station, gravity felt wrong after three days under steady thrust.
The deck didn’t move, didn’t hum with the faint, reassuring vibration of engines working. It just sat there, solid and indifferent. Mosby’s stride shortened without his permission, knees flexing a little more than needed as if the floor might shift. He didn’t bother trying to hide it.
Sael adjusted within a dozen steps. Her balance settled, shoulders squaring as though she had changed environments by choice rather than necessity.
They stopped first at the relay office, a narrow room tucked between a supply depot and a fuel broker. The air smelled faintly of hot circuitry and old paper.
The clerk behind the counter barely looked up. “Name?”
“Mosby. Trader.”
She tapped a key, reached into a tray without turning her head, and slid a thin stack of printed slips across the counter. “Three inbound. Two pending transmission confirmation.”
Mosby picked them up and stepped aside. The slips were thin, cheap paper—something about physical copies still made people feel better when signals went bad.
Short notes. Route confirmations. A polite complaint about a pickup delay he would have to smooth over later.
If later came, he reminded himself.
Nothing else.
Sael watched him read, her hands folded loosely behind her back. “You were expecting something?”
“Not really,” he said, folding the slips once and tucking them into his jacket. “Just checking who’s still talking.”
They ate in a dockside restaurant that specialized in food meant to remind travelers of somewhere else.
The lighting was warm and low, the walls decorated with faded prints of coastlines and farms from half a dozen worlds. The air carried a blend of spices that didn’t quite belong together but tried very hard to pretend they did.
Sael tried a local stew and decided, after two careful bites, that it was attempting three cuisines at once and succeeding at none of them. She ate it anyway, methodically, as though it were a task that required completion.
Mosby stuck with bread, roasted meat, and something that claimed to be beer. He drank it without comment, which was comment enough.
They sat near a wide panoramic viewport overlooking one of the outer docking arms. Traffic drifted past in slow, deliberate motion: tugs nudging cargo modules, a shuttle rotating on thrusters before easing toward a berth, running lights sliding across the glass like distant stars that had forgotten how to stay still.
Mosby’s gaze kept straying back to the port.
Not staring. Just… checking.
Sael noticed on the third time. “You’re expecting someone to wave?”
“Wouldn’t mind a friendly face,” he said.
His eyes shifted again, tracking a vessel easing out from behind a lattice of gantries. For a moment all he saw was reflected glare—then a dark hull line, angular, unadorned, slipping sideways under maneuvering jets.
It might have been the same ship.
Might not.
By the time he leaned forward slightly, trying to catch a registry mark, a fuel tender drifted between it and the viewport. When the view cleared, the ship was already turning away, vanishing behind the curve of the station’s outer ring.
He sat back.
Sael set her spoon down quietly. “Problem?”
“Next stop is Karamar,” he said, as if she had asked something else. “Then New Horizon. Traffic will be heavier there. Good place to disappear in a crowd.”
“Is that possible in the Star, the way she’s painted?” she asked lightly.
He gave her a sour look over the rim of his mug.
She held his gaze a moment longer, the faintest hint of a smile at the corner of her mouth. “You think we’re being followed.”
“I think something took an interest,” he said. “That’s not the same thing. Yet.”
She didn’t push further. But when she lifted her spoon again, she didn’t taste the stew. She was looking past her reflection in the viewport, into the slow dance of ships beyond the glass.
Departure from Aureline was clean. Gate traffic was steady but tightly controlled, ships spaced at precise intervals, each holding its assigned vector like freight cars in an invisible train.
Sael leaned forward slightly as the gate complex grew in the forward ports.
It was not a simple structure.
Two immense rectangular loops hung in space, joined at the middle by a thick structural spine. Trusses, emitter rails, and armored conduits laced the framework, giving the whole construct the look of industrial machinery built on a scale that dwarfed warships. One loop handled departures, the other arrivals, their fields kept separate by dense hardware at the narrow waist between them.
Faint distortions shimmered along sections of the framework, like heat haze over metal, where containment fields pressed against the vacuum. Thin bands of polarized light slid along emitter tracks as the system constantly recalibrated.
Ships ahead approached the outbound lobe one at a time. Each reached an invisible boundary, wavered in a soft gravitational shimmer—
—and vanished.
“First time through a major gate from the front seat?” Mosby asked, eyes on the approach display.
“I’ve used them,” Sael said. “Never from up here.”
“Then don’t blink.”
The flight computer chimed as it accepted the gate’s approach envelope. Thrusters whispered in short, precise bursts, trimming their vector by fractions too small for human hands to manage cleanly.
A calm voice came over the comm.
“Outbound vessel Farther Star, confirm mass profile one-one-eight thousand metric and lane assignment Delta-Seven.”
Mosby thumbed the transmit. “Farther Star confirms. Delta-Seven.”
“Lane lock verified. Maintain envelope. You are cleared to threshold on mark.”
The console tone shifted — subtle, but final. Lane confirmation pulsed green. The ship was no longer navigating toward the gate. It was being guided into alignment with it.
“Crossing threshold,” Mosby said quietly.
The forward view did not flare or flash.
Instead, the stars thinned, smeared—
—and were gone.
For an instant there was a hollow sensation, not in her body but in her sense of distance, as if the universe had quietly stepped back.
Then the tunnel resolved around them.
They were inside a corridor of structured spacetime. Layered geometries flowed past in vast, slow patterns — planes of shifting color, lattices folding through one another, curves that seemed to rotate without turning. Nothing repeated. Nothing held still long enough to be named.
Sael’s fingers tightened on the armrests before she noticed. “I thought it would be… more dramatic.”
“Be glad it isn’t,” Mosby said. “Dramatic means something’s wrong.”
Gate transit felt nothing like direct FTL. There was no engine push, no sense of acceleration. Instead, it was as if the ship had been caught in a current far larger than itself, carried along a channel carved through the fabric of space. The controls showed no meaningful authority. They were passengers now.
Ahead, faint and distant, another ship rode the same lane. Its navigation lights held at a constant separation, neither growing nor shrinking.
To their right, something flared.
A streak of blue-white light tore past in a neighboring transit stream, Doppler-shifted into a hard ultraviolet glare that left a brief afterimage on Sael’s vision before it vanished into the flowing geometry.
She blinked. “What was that?”
“Another lane,” Mosby said. “Different mass class, different transit rate. Gates run a lot of streams side by side. We only ever see the edges.”
The Star hummed steadily, but the sound came from its own systems rather than propulsion. Field stabilizers murmured. Air fans whispered. Inside the hull, everything was ordinary.
Outside, reality had been turned into infrastructure.
“This time,” Mosby added, “there’s even less to do than in FTL.”
The navigation computer did not steer. It merely confirmed they remained centered in their assigned flow. Deviation wasn’t permitted; maneuver wasn’t possible.
Transit time to the far gate: fifteen days.
Long enough for routine to settle in. Life-support filters would need changing. Mosby ran diagnostics on the secondary heat exchangers. Minor maintenance, small tasks to mark the passing days.
On the fourteenth day of transit, they sat together in the cockpit, watching the slow drift of structured light beyond the forward ports. The flowing geometry of the corridor never repeated, never resolved into anything the eye could hold for long. It was like staring into a river made of mathematics.
Sael let the quiet stretch before she spoke.
“What is it between you and Korrin?” she asked. “Why do you hate each other so much?”
Mosby didn’t answer right away. His eyes stayed on the instruments, though the ship required nothing from him now.
“I started out as just another assault pilot for the Marines,” he said at last. “We were on one of those planets I can’t even remember the name of anymore, flying evac. One of our battalions got cut off in a mountain valley. The Wards had the area thick with anti-air, and our people were getting picked off trying to move the wounded.”
Sael’s brow furrowed slightly. “Wards?”
Mosby didn’t look at her. “What we called them. We were Marines, Soldiers, Sailors. They called their troops Stewards.”
A small pause.
“It pissed them off when we shortened it.”
The shifting light from the corridor slid across the canopy and over his face, flattening the lines there.
“I went in low and as fast as I could push the shuttle,” he went on. “Took fire on the way down, but I got the first load out. They were all in bad shape. I went back again, but the shuttle had problems and hit hard on landing. I swapped birds and went back in.”
He shifted slightly in his seat, one shoulder rolling as if easing an old stiffness that never quite went away.
“I made three more runs. Lost two more shuttles. Last time in, I had shrapnel in my side and didn’t know it. Collapsed after we got clear.”
“Sounds bad,” she said.
Mosby gave a small nod. “We did what we had to. Most didn’t make it out, though.”
The corridor flowed on outside, indifferent.
“When I got out of the hospital, I’d been transferred. Korrin was CO of a special operations unit. Wanted me specifically because I’d been crazy enough—and good enough—for the kind of missions he was handing out.”
His mouth twitched, not quite a smile.
“I didn’t like being pulled off evac for covert ops that seemed pointless at the time. Though I did get to fly the Star.”
Sael turned toward him. “You flew this ship in the war?”
“Sure did. She was something then.” A faint smile touched his mouth, but it didn’t last. “Toward the end, the war was winding down. There was a team behind the contact line. Good people. I’d gotten them in and out of bad places more than once.”
He looked down at his hands for a moment, flexing his fingers as if remembering the weight of a flight stick.
“Korrin got orders to pull out. Evac the planet. The team didn’t make the list. Acceptable losses.”
Sael didn’t interrupt.
“I couldn’t do it,” Mosby said quietly. “I walked into the command post, argued with him, and when that didn’t work…” He gave a small shrug. “I decked him.”
Her eyebrows rose despite herself.
“Took the Star and went back for them. By the time I got back, Korrin had pulled the unit out. We were left on the planet—me, the team, and a ship that wasn’t supposed to be there anymore.”
The corridor’s light shifted again, slow and distant.

Around them, ships traced slow arcs of light and motion — freighters heavy with cargo, couriers darting between lanes, a passenger liner gliding inward with stately precision. Every movement logged. Timed. Expected.
Sael sat in the copilot’s chair, hands folded loosely in her lap, gaze forward. The station lights played faint reflections across the canopy glass. She had not spoken since returning. A little too composed.
Ted stood behind them, one hand braced on the bulkhead as he watched the readouts over Mosby’s shoulder.
The Farther Star cleared the immediate traffic envelope and drifted outward under minimal thrust. The station fell slowly behind, its rotating arms bright against the planet’s curve. Beyond it, the stars sharpened into hard points of light.
“Farther Star,” control said, “you are entering navigation lane Aurelia departure. Stand by for queue assignment.”
The ship slid into the outbound flow, one more icon among dozens edging toward their respective gates.
A pause. A soft carrier hiss.
“Farther Star, you are number twelve in the Aurelia Gate departure queue. Hold position and await sequencing.”
“Twelve,” Mosby repeated after muting the channel.
The Farther Star drifted in the navigation lane, holds full, waiting for departure clearance and its place in the outbound queue — just another ship in a system built on schedules and predictable movement.
Ted made a low, thoughtful sound. “That is enough time,” he said.
“For what?” Sael asked.
Ted did not answer.
Mosby was watching the other ships as they began to queue.
Transponder icons slid into ordered lanes on the display, each tagged with registry, mass, destination. The system’s quiet choreography unfolded exactly as designed — predictable, regulated, safe.
Then one contact caught his eye.
“That one.” Mosby leaned forward and pointed at a marker nearing the departure threshold. “Does that look familiar to you?”
Sael leaned closer to his display. For a moment she said nothing. Then her breath caught.
“At Aureline,” she said softly.
Mosby nodded once. “Yeah. Thought so.”
The contact tag resolved as the system refreshed — registry masked, drive signature blurred into the noise floor. It matched the way it had looked before: present, but unwilling to be seen.
The ship ahead of them brightened as its drive came up under sequencing control, nudged toward the gate in a clean, obedient arc.
Mosby’s voice dropped, steady and matter-of-fact. “When it enters the gate, we break out of lane and file the second flight plan.”
Sael’s eyes stayed on the display, tracking the other ship’s slow glide toward the luminous geometry of the gate. “They’ll see the vector change.”
“There are a couple of ships between us and them,” Mosby said. “Once they cross the threshold, they’re committed. They have to complete transit before they can even think about turning around.”
Sael understood immediately. That would put them far ahead by the time anyone on the other side could even begin to guess where they had gone.
The distant ship crossed the outer approach boundary, its transponder pulsing brighter as the gate’s field began to build around it.
A faint vibration thrummed through the deck — not from their own drives, but from the gate cycling up for transit.
“Timing will be critical,” Ted said.
Mosby flexed his fingers once over the controls. “Good thing we’re not in a hurry,” he said.
Ahead of them, the gate shimmered, distortion spreading like heat haze across the stars as spacetime bent around the departing vessel.
Mosby’s thumb hovered over the authorization tab for the second flight plan. Waiting.
Ted glanced at his display, claws moving lightly across the interface. His head tilted.
“Friend Mosby,” he said, “the filed destination is not accurate.”
Mosby didn’t look away from the canopy. “Nope.”
Ted studied the numbers a moment longer. “It indicates Dawn Hold.”
“Sure does.”
Sael’s eyes flicked toward him. “You’re putting a false destination on record.”
“Just keeping paperwork happy,” Mosby said. “If anyone checks, we were heading somewhere nice and ordinary. If our friends out there check, they’ll be looking for us in the wrong places.”
Ted made a quiet sound of understanding. “A decoy trail.”
“That’s the idea.”
The gate’s glow intensified ahead of them as the departing ship slid deeper into the field.
Sael’s voice stayed low. “No traffic control. No one knows where we are.”
“No one coming if it goes wrong,” Ted added quietly.
Mosby gave a single nod. “Yeah,” he said. “That’s the idea.”
The ship ahead — the one they had seen at Aureline — vanished in a flare of distorted light.
“Now,” Mosby said.
His thumb came down on the console.
The second flight plan transmitted — clean, routine, logged for Dawn Hold.
Then his other hand moved.
The Farther Star rolled hard out of the navigation lane.
Warning tones chirped as proximity buffers protested. Transponder traffic spiked — automated queries, routing challenges, system confusion rippling through the local net.
Mosby ignored it all.
“Vector plotted,” Ted said, voice suddenly sharp. “Window is narrow.”
“I see it.”
The ship’s nose swung toward a gap between traffic flows — not the gate corridor, not any declared route, but open black where no outbound vessel was supposed to be accelerating.
“Inertial dampers to full,” Mosby snapped.
“Dampers holding.”
The drive began to spool, a rising hum building through the deck plates and into their bones.
Behind them, the gate flared again as the next ship in sequence advanced, its transit wash blooming bright across sensors. For a few critical seconds, every watchful system would be focused forward.
“Field is building,” Ted said.
Sael gripped the edge of her seat, eyes fixed ahead. “Commit.”
Mosby didn’t hesitate. “Jump.”
Space folded.
Stars flared white and vanished as the Farther Star drove into FTL, slipping free of the ordered lanes and their watching systems. No one spoke.
The cockpit lights flickered while the field geometry settled. Numbers streamed across the panels. The harsh shudder of entry eased into a deep, steady vibration that lived in the bones more than the ears. The sound of it wasn’t loud so much as intimate — a pressure in the teeth, a tremor in the seat frame, the ship’s mass agreeing, reluctantly, to be somewhere it had no business being.
“Field integrity holding,” Ted said. “Thermal load within margin.”
Mosby gave a short nod but kept his hands near the controls. He didn’t fly the ship so much as stand ready to catch it if it slipped.
Sael rose and moved to the navigation console. She had known they were bypassing the gates. That had been clear the moment Mosby broke from the departure queue.
Direct to Sol. Risky, but reasonable.
She studied the projected course.
Her eyes narrowed.
The display was alive with interference — shadows forming and dissolving, vectors bending where no mass should have been. Data ghosts flickered in and out of solution. The predictive line refused to stay straight; it wandered, corrected, wandered again, as though the computer were arguing with the space ahead.
“What is this?” she asked.
“Gravimetric instabilities,” Ted said. “Radiation shear. Stellar fragment fields.”
Mosby kept his gaze forward. “Shortest line between here and Sol. Most ships don’t take it. Most ships aren’t trying to disappear.”
Ted tilted his head slightly. “Most ships,” he said, “also prefer not to die.”
The deck trembled faintly as the field compensated for uneven space ahead, then lurched. It wasn’t a shove so much as a momentary absence of support, like a stair step that wasn’t where you expected it to be. Sael’s fingers tightened on the console edge before she could stop them.
Ted adjusted a control by a fractional degree. “Stress variance elevated,” he said. “Still tolerable.” His voice stayed mild, but his hands did not; they moved with the precise hurry of a man keeping a flame from spreading.
Sael studied the distorted starfield a moment longer. The wavering threads of light ahead no longer seemed distant or abstract. They suggested motion without pattern, structure without stability. A place that looked calm until you tried to lay a ruler across it.
For the first time since the jump, unease stirred beneath her composure.
The urgency of escape faded. In its place came the long, suspended tension of a difficult crossing — the kind where the danger lay not behind them, but ahead.
The ship rode the hum of FTL like a small boat on a long, invisible swell. Beyond the forward ports, space was no longer space but a soft distortion — stars stretched into faint, wavering threads that slid past without ever seeming to move. Sometimes the threads thickened, brightening as if the field were scraping too close to something massive and unseen; sometimes they thinned, leaving the glass looking almost empty. Mosby watched those changes the way a sailor watches the texture of water.
The cockpit lights were dimmed for long watch. Instrument panels cast steady color across the consoles and the tired faces gathered there. Time in transit did what it always did: it made the ship smaller, the air drier, the muscles stiffer, the mind sharper in some places and duller in others.
Sael stood behind the pilot’s chair, one hand resting lightly on the back of it, bracing against the subtle turbulence.
“There is something you should know,” she said.
Mosby didn’t turn. “That usually means I’m not going to like it.”
Ted’s gaze shifted toward her, reflective eyes catching the panel glow.
Sael took a breath. “About who might be reading our traffic. About why I didn’t say anything sooner.”
Now Mosby glanced back. “Figured you’d get around to it.” His tone was casual; his eyes weren’t.
“There’s a group,” she said. “They don’t hold territory. They don’t fly under a flag. But when they take an interest in a system… things change.”
Ted made a soft sound of recognition.
“Got a name?” Mosby asked.
“Intelligence says they call themselves Ascendants. They believe civilizations evolve through pressure,” Sael continued. “That stagnation is more dangerous than upheaval. If a society is shielded from hardship too long, it weakens. Collapses later. Worse.”
“So they let the bad things happen,” Mosby said.
“Sometimes they remove the things preventing it,” Sael replied, lowering her head. “Sometimes they cause them.”
Ted tilted his head. “They attempt to restore what they consider natural selection at scale.”
Mosby let out a low breath. “People die.”
“Yes,” Sael said softly. No defense. No excuse. The admission landed without flourish, which made it heavier.
“They see war, famine, collapse as a fire,” she said. “Something that tempers what survives. If a civilization can’t endure that, they believe it wasn’t viable to begin with.”
Silence settled in the cockpit, heavier than before. Even the ship seemed to listen, the steady vibration continuing beneath their feet like a reminder that survival was never abstract out here.
“And you?” Mosby asked. “You buy that?”
Sael watched the warped starlight ahead. “I understand how someone could. That’s not the same as agreeing.”
“They mistake resilience for worth,” Ted said softly.
“They break things to see what survives,” Mosby muttered, “like children knocking down their toys.”
No one argued.
After a moment, Mosby leaned back in his seat. He rolled his shoulders once, as if settling himself under a weight he’d decided to carry anyway.
“This means we stop acting like we’re in traffic. Ted?”
Ted straightened slightly. “Yes, Friend Mosby.”
“How ugly is it going to be to push the field harder than she likes?”
Ted hesitated. “Inadvisable. Inefficient. Structurally impolite to the drive.” His eyes flicked to a temperature band that was already inching upward.
Mosby’s mouth twitched. “But possible.”
Ted studied him. “What do you intend to do, Friend Mosby?”
Mosby kept his eyes forward. “I’m tired of people breathing down our necks. Tired of being one step behind.”
His fingers tapped lightly on the console. Not impatience — counting.
“We can’t change lanes,” he said. “But we can open the throttle. Put some distance between us and whoever’s watching.”
Sael frowned. “You’re talking about overdriving the field.”
“I’m talking about getting there first.”
Ted considered that. “The stress load will not be trivial in this space. Worse could happen in this region.”
“Neither is being hunted,” Mosby said.
A quiet moment passed, filled with the steady hum of the drive. The ship answered the silence with a faint, restless shiver, as if it disliked being discussed in front of strangers.
Then Ted inclined his head. “Very well. I will attempt to persuade the drive to cooperate.”
Mosby gave a faint smile. “That’s why you’re here.”
He reached for the controls, beginning the careful process of nudging the field parameters beyond their comfortable margins. Not a shove — a series of small thefts from the safety envelope, each one bought with heat and wear.
Without looking at her, he added, “You’d better be straight with us, Sael.”
She stiffened slightly. “I am.”
“I knew you weren’t telling me everything,” he said. “But this? This is the kind of thing I should’ve known up front.”
Sael held his gaze. Her voice, when she spoke, was calm — and distant.
“I can only tell you what I’m authorized to. And when it becomes necessary.”
Ted’s eyes moved between them, silent.
Mosby looked at her for a long moment. Measuring. Reassessing. The way a pilot judges weather: not by what it is, but by what it’s about to become.
“Fine,” he said at last.
The word wasn’t agreement. It was a line being drawn.
“We’ll play it that way.”
Sael inclined her head once, accepting the terms — and the cost.
The hum of the drive deepened as Ted fed more power into the field. The vibration changed pitch, climbing into something tighter and less forgiving, and the hull answered with a low complaint that came up through the soles of their feet.
The jump hadn’t changed.
But the way they were riding it had the ship shuddering under strain.
And something between them, once easy and unspoken, now carried weight.
A hairline crack.
Not broken.
Not yet.
But there.
Time stretched.
The Farther Star drove on through the warped dark, her engines carrying that deeper, strained tone that never quite faded now. The ship felt alive underfoot — not in the easy, balanced way of a clean run, but like something enduring a burden it would rather not bear. Panels stayed loose. Tools stayed out. The air smelled faintly of hot insulation and recycled water.
Watches turned over quietly. Rarely were less than two in the cockpit at once. Ted moved through the ship in restless circuits, opening panels, tightening tolerances, making minute adjustments before small complaints could grow into serious ones. He ate standing up. Slept in snatches with one hand still on a diagnostic lead.
Sael spent long hours at the navigation console, silent, watching the projected corridor ahead worsen by degrees. The farther they went, the less the charts meant. She learned the ship’s new language: the slight flicker in a readout that meant nothing now but would mean everything ten minutes later; the tiny delay between a tremor and Ted’s correction.
Mosby stayed in the pilot’s chair more often than he slept. When he did rest, it was shallow and brief. When awake, he watched the forward distortion or the readouts — and not Sael. He accepted food and drink without looking away, as if turning his head might invite the universe to take advantage.
They were nearly halfway through the crossing when the rhythm changed.
Ted felt it through the deck before the instruments confirmed it. A subtle shift in vibration. A wrongness in the engine note. The hum lost its steady center and began to “hunt,” wavering as if the field could not decide where to hold.
He was already leaning over the console when the first numbers began to drift.
Ahead, the distorted geometry tightened.
The smooth lensing of FTL twisted into knots.
The ship didn’t shudder.
It slammed.
A brutal lateral shock tore through the hull, hard enough to rattle teeth and snap heads sideways. Consoles flared white, then red, as field harmonics ripped out of alignment. Somewhere aft, something heavy broke loose with a metallic crash. A second sound followed — lighter, faster — tools or fittings skittering across deck plates.
Sael was thrown into the console edge. Pain flared along her shoulder as she caught herself too late. Her breath came sharp and fast, discipline cracking under the sudden violence.
Ted staggered, caught himself, and lunged for the controls, claws scraping metal as he dumped load from one emitter bank to another before the cascade could run away. Warnings overlapped: thermal, drift, emitter imbalance, dampers protesting at their limit.
The readouts went wild.
Gravitational variance spiked off scale. Radiation noise screamed across the sensors. The navigation display filled with phantom returns — ghost masses blooming and collapsing like reflections in shattered glass.
The Farther Star yawed hard. Inertial dampers whined in protest. A second impact followed, upward this time, a gut-wrenching heave that made the deck seem to fall away beneath them. For an instant Sael’s stomach rose as if gravity had forgotten its job.
Sael dropped to one knee, hands braced against the plating. Her breathing lost rhythm, each inhale a sharp pull as the scale of what they had entered finally broke through her training.
Mosby did not fight the motion.
He rode it.
Hands steady on the controls, he let the ship run before the storm the way a seasoned captain runs before a hurricane swell — never trying to outmuscle the sea, only keeping the bow aligned with the least deadly path. He fed small, measured corrections into the helm, feeling the rhythm beneath the chaos. His eyes were half on the instruments and half on the way the distortion “leaned,” reading it by instinct the way he once read smoke and tracers.
Ahead, the distortion coiled into a visible rupture — a warped fold in the flow, like a breaking wave frozen in space.
They drove straight into it.
Ted cut power sharply, bleeding energy into the lateral stabilizers. The engine note dropped to a strained, uneven growl.
The rupture hit.
The ship convulsed. A deep, tearing groan rolled through the structure, felt more than heard. Every light in the cockpit died for a fraction of a second, leaving only motion and the sickening sensation of falling sideways through nothing.
Then the backups caught.
Dim emergency lighting snapped on. Systems clawed their way back into coherence. The violent motion eased to heavy turbulence… then tremors… then the deep, labored hum of an overworked drive. The smell of overheated circuitry drifted from somewhere behind the panels, sharp and chemical.
Numbers crawled back from the edge.
Radiation noise fell. Phantom contacts thinned and vanished. The twisted knot of distortion ahead slowly unwound into the familiar, uneasy flow of FTL.
Ted stayed bent over the console, breathing hard as he made one last, delicate adjustment.
“She’s still with us,” he said quietly.
Sael pushed herself upright, face pale, composure stripped down to effort alone. She kept her eyes on the forward glass as if looking away would make it happen again.
Mosby kept his eyes forward, hands light on the controls, listening to the ship the way a sailor listens to a hull in heavy seas.
They had not gone around the mountain.
They had just run before the storm — and lived.
The last tremor faded into the deeper, uneven hum of the drive.
“Ted,” Mosby said sharply.
“Already going, Friend Mosby.”
Ted was in motion as he spoke, hands moving across the controls, isolating stressed systems and easing the field back into a sustainable geometry. The warning tones diminished, one by one, until only a low diagnostic murmur remained.
Mosby glanced sideways. “You all right, Sael?”
She was still braced against the console where the shock had thrown her. One hand gripped the edge hard enough to whiten her knuckles. Her breathing was steadying, but not yet steady.
“I’m fine,” she said, and this time she almost made it sound true.
She straightened slowly, rolling her shoulder once as if testing it, then returned her attention to the navigation display. Her posture was controlled again, movements deliberate — but a fraction too deliberate.
Ted straightened from his console. “Drive integrity remains within operational tolerance,” he said. “She has endured worse.”
Mosby allowed himself the faintest nod. The Farther Star had seen combat, hard burns, and things no shipping registry would ever hear about. It would take more than one bad stretch of space to break her.
Maybe.
He looked forward again, hands light on the controls, listening to the ship. The vibration had changed — rougher, but stable. A hull with stories always sounded that way.
Then, without turning his head, he let his gaze shift just enough to catch Sael in the reflection of the forward glass.
She was staring at the distorted starfield as if it had personally offended her. Her jaw was set, but he saw the delay in her reactions, the careful stillness of someone forcing control back into place.
She wasn’t used to this kind of space.
He said nothing.
Didn’t soften his tone. Didn’t offer reassurance. Didn’t call attention to it.
He just logged it away, the way a captain notes a change in weather or a new sound in the rigging.
Something to remember.
Outside, the warped starlight flowed on, vast and indifferent. The Star drove forward through it, battered but unbowed. And inside the cockpit, the silence carried a little more weight than it had before.
Mosby kept his eyes forward a moment longer, then spoke without looking at her.
“Why don’t you have the autodoc run a scan,” he said. “No point finding out later you cracked something.”
Sael started to wave it off, then stopped. She gave a small nod instead.
“Good idea,” she said quietly.
She stepped out of the cockpit with careful control, not rushing, not limping — but not moving quite as easily as before.
Mosby watched her go only long enough to make sure she cleared the hatch without stumbling.
Ted was already pulling up engineering schematics, his brow plates drawn tight in concentration.
“I want to check engineering,” he said. “Some of the structural readings are… concerning.”
Mosby grunted. “Go.”
Ted paused at the hatch and looked back.
“Friend Mosby, warn me if there is another gravity sheer.”
Mosby gave a short nod. “You’ll hear it before I do.”
Ted inclined his head and disappeared down the passage at a quick, efficient pace.
Mosby was alone in the cockpit now. He rested his hands lightly on the controls and listened to the ship — to the vibration in the deck, the pitch of the engines, the faint groans of metal under stress.
The Farther Star sounded tired.
But she was still running.
The corridor lights seemed brighter than usual.
Sael told herself it was only the contrast after the dim cockpit, but she didn’t slow her pace until she reached the small medical bay aft of the galley. The hatch slid open with a soft pneumatic sigh.
The autodoc cradle waited where it always did, indifferent and patient.
She sat on the edge of it and let out a breath she hadn’t realized she was still holding.
Her hands were steady now. That surprised her. It irritated her a little, too — as if steadiness were supposed to mean she hadn’t been frightened, and she knew better than to confuse the two.
She rolled her shoulder carefully. A dull ache flared, nothing sharp. Nothing broken. Just the memory of the impact lingering in muscle and bone.
“Begin scan,” she said.
The machine hummed to life, cool light passing slowly over her. She stared at the ceiling while it worked, listening to the distant vibration of the drive through the deck. Somewhere forward, very far away in the ship’s geometry, Mosby was still holding a line through unstable space. She could feel that effort as a constant pressure, like a hand placed lightly at the base of the spine.
She had read reports about corridors like this. Hazard zones. Unstable regions. Statistical anomalies.
Words on a screen.
None of them had prepared her for the way space had moved — not empty, not passive, but violent and unpredictable, as if the universe itself had shrugged and nearly thrown them off.
She closed her eyes for a moment.
Training said to catalog the fear. Name it. Reduce it to information.
Her pulse had spiked. Breathing irregular. Cognitive narrowing under sudden threat.
All manageable.
Still, when the ship had lurched the second time, she had not thought of protocols or contingencies.
She had thought, very clearly:
This is how people vanish.
The autodoc chimed softly, announcing the end of its scan.
“Minor soft tissue trauma,” it reported in its calm, neutral voice. “No fractures. No internal bleeding. Recommend rest and anti-inflammatory.”
“I’ll take the medication,” she said. “Skip the rest.”
The dispenser clicked, offering a small capsule. She swallowed it dry and sat there a moment longer, hands folded loosely in her lap. The capsule tasted faintly of chalk and citrus, as if the designers wanted pain relief to feel polite.
Mosby had told her to come here without making it an order.
She knew what that meant.
He had seen.
She wasn’t sure whether that comforted her or unsettled her more.
After a while, she slid off the cradle and straightened her jacket. Her expression, when she caught it in the reflection of a darkened panel, was composed again.
Not the same as before.
But steady enough.
She dimmed the lights in the bay before leaving, letting the quiet hum of the autodoc fade behind her as she made her way back toward the cockpit. The ship felt different in the passageways now — still tense, still strained, but no longer being physically shaken apart. The deck no longer tried to buck her. The vibration had settled into a tired, persistent thrum, like an animal that had stopped fighting and was simply enduring.
As she moved forward, she passed an open access panel where someone — Ted, almost certainly — had left a tool kit half-unpacked. A strip of diagnostic tape fluttered with the ship’s breath of recycled air. The smell of warm insulation was still there, but it was fading, diluted by the ordinary odors of metal, lubricant, and stale coffee.
By the time she reached the cockpit hatch, the emergency lights had been cut back and the main illumination restored to its low watch setting. The panels still wore their amber cautions like bruises, but the screaming alarms were gone.
Mosby let out a slow breath he hadn’t realized he was holding. He did it through his nose, carefully, like a man afraid that if he exhaled too hard the universe would notice and take another swing. “We still in one piece?”
“Yes,” Ted said. A beat. “Define ‘comfortably.’”
“Later,” Mosby muttered. His hands stayed on the controls, but the grip had loosened; he could afford to let the ship fly for a few seconds without expecting it to betray him. “Where are we?”
Sael leaned forward, studying the forward display as the sensor returns stabilized. The distortion outside the ports was still there, but it had smoothed. The threads of warped light no longer kinked and snapped; they ran in long, uneasy curves, as if the corridor had finally decided to behave.
The view resolved into a dense tapestry of motion. Not the violent, impossible clutter from the storm’s heart, but real traffic now — old lanes, stray debris, radiation blooms that pulsed and faded like heat lightning. The kind of mess a busy, dangerous region accumulated over decades. The kind of mess you could navigate, if you respected it.
Mosby’s eyes flicked once to the chronometer. Long enough. Not forever. He shifted in the chair, spine cracking faintly, and for the first time in hours let his shoulders drop.
Ted was already moving again, quiet in the background, making notes, rebalancing loads, treating the Farther Star the way you treated a battered machine you loved: with blunt honesty and careful hands.
And Sael, looking at the now-legible chaos ahead, understood the difference. The storm had been something that could kill you without meaning to. What lay in front of them was worse in a different way. It could kill you because it didn’t care at all — and because people had been careless here, long before they arrived.
Sol.
The inner system was alive with traffic.
Dozens—no, hundreds—of transponder signatures threaded across the display, moving in layered corridors of light and velocity that stacked over and under one another in disciplined patterns. Gate flares winked in and out of existence around the system’s periphery, each arrival and departure briefly bending starlight into impossible angles before snapping back to normal, the distortions so common here they barely warranted a second glance.
Freight convoys crawled along assigned lanes like glowing beads on invisible wires, mass and momentum carefully managed by systems that had been refined for centuries. Passenger liners glided in stately arcs toward orbital hubs large enough to be cities in their own right, their approach vectors adjusted by fractions of a degree to avoid ripples that could propagate for thousands of kilometers. Military patrol craft held quiet positions near the major junctions, their transponders crisp and unmistakable—present not to threaten, but to remind.
And threaded through it all was the constant murmur of traffic control. Overlapping channels of clearances, advisories, routing adjustments—voices calm, precise, endlessly patient. The sound of a system that never slept because it could not afford to.
Sael exhaled softly. “All gates lead to Sol.”
“Yeah,” Mosby said. “And everybody shows up eventually.”
Ted tilted his head, watching the traffic density map update as new arrivals folded seamlessly into the flow. “We are not conspicuous,” he observed.
“Let’s keep it that way,” Mosby replied.
He nudged the maneuvering controls, easing the Farther Star’s velocity down into the lower end of the local traffic band. The ship settled into the stream without protest, one more tired freighter among hundreds of others that had crossed too much distance and seen too much vacuum. Here, even exhaustion had a category.
“File a minor systems stress report,” Mosby said. “Nothing dramatic. We’re a hauler that pushed her drive too hard and needs a breather. That’s a story Sol hears every hour.”
Sael’s hands moved over the comm panel, her voice steady and professional as she transmitted the report. “Requesting low-priority inspection window and temporary berth. No hazardous cargo. No passenger services required.”
Ted glanced at the drive readouts again, eyes lingering on a stubborn amber indicator. “A pressurized berth would be… appreciated.”
Mosby gave him a sideways look. “You mean for the ship, right?”
Ted’s gaze shifted toward him. “Primarily.”
Traffic control responded a moment later, voice calm, almost bored. A berth assignment. A projected delay measured in hours, not days. A routing corridor to follow, already overlaid on their display.
Routine. Unremarkable.
Exactly what Mosby wanted.
The Farther Star slid deeper into the system, swallowed by the organized chaos of humanity’s busiest crossroads. Behind them, the FTL jump—the gravity shear, the storm—was already just another line item in a log that would be archived, indexed, and forgotten.
Ahead, five gates burned steadily, each one a controlled wound in spacetime leading somewhere else. Old gates. New gates. Gates rebuilt after wars, expanded after famines, reinforced after mistakes no one wanted to repeat.
“Sol’s Grand Central Gates,” Mosby remarked. “More tonnage goes through there in a day than most systems see in a year.”
He watched the flow of ships around them, hands light on the controls.
“Stay small,” he said quietly. “Stay boring.”
Sael glanced at him, then back out at the impossible sprawl of motion and light. For the first time since leaving New Horizon, she allowed herself to believe no one was chasing them.
For the moment, they were just another ship in the crowd.
And Mosby knew that was as close to safe as the galaxy ever got.
The berth wasn’t glamorous.
A rotating service drum bolted onto the spine of one of Sol’s older orbital yards, its interior lined with docking cradles that had seen decades of tired freighters and overworked haulers. The Farther Star settled into one of them with a soft, hollow thud that carried through the deck as the clamps engaged.
External power umbilicals extended and locked into place. A moment later, the ship’s systems sighed as station load replaced strain, the constant tension in the deck easing just enough to be felt.
“Finally,” Mosby muttered.
Ted was already moving before the dock seals finished cycling.
By the time Mosby reached engineering, two access panels were open and a diagnostic lead snaked deep into the drive housing. The smell of hot metal and polymer sealant hung in the air, sharp and familiar—damage, but manageable.
“How bad?” Mosby asked, leaning in the hatchway with a mug of something that claimed to be coffee.
Ted did not look up. “We remain spaceworthy,” he said. “Define ‘proudly.’”
Mosby grunted. “You’ll tell me when it gets expensive.”
“It already is.”
Mosby reached past him to tap a readout. “You gonna need help?”
Ted paused, considering the display as if the answer might still change. “Yes,” he said simply.
He keyed a short transmission, voice low and precise.
Within an hour, three Talari arrived.
They came without uniforms, insignia, or visible affiliation. One carried a tool case older than the Star. Another had a sling of diagnostic filaments looped over one shoulder like climbing rope. The third moved with the careful gait of someone whose joints had been replaced more than once and expected to be replaced again.
They did not introduce themselves.
They did not need to.
Ted inclined his head in greeting. Subtle shifts of posture passed between them—information exchanged faster than language, acknowledgments without ceremony.
Mosby watched from the corridor. “You just… call people?”
“I indicated assistance would be efficient,” Ted said.
“And they just show up?”
“Yes.”
“Do they want paying?”
Ted considered. “Not in the way you mean.”
The Talari slipped into the open compartments with quiet competence. One began recalibrating a warped field bracket. Another laid fresh sealant mesh along a stressed conduit. The third connected to the diagnostic bus and began reading logs, eyes unfocusing briefly as data streamed through him.
They worked without chatter. But now and then one would pause, head tilting slightly—receiving, considering, passing something along.
Sael noticed.
“They’re sharing,” she said quietly.
Ted glanced at her. “We are not good at keeping what we learn to ourselves.”
Mosby folded his arms. “About the ship, I hope.”
Ted did not answer immediately.
“Primarily,” he said at last.
Mosby left them to it.
In the galley, he scrolled through the delayed message queue. Most of it was routing chatter and automated confirmations that had tried to follow them along standard trade paths. A few invoices. One mildly irritated note from a broker wondering why a shipment acknowledgment had originated from the wrong system.
No new contracts. No fresh jobs.
“Shortcut,” he muttered. “Saves time, costs everything else.”
He closed the display and pulled on a jacket that had seen more ports than he had.
Sael found him at the hatch.
“Going somewhere?” she asked.
“Salt broker,” he said. “Renew the Helion. Then food. Real food. I’m not eating another tube of protein medley unless it apologizes.”
She hesitated only a moment. “I’ll come.”
Mosby shrugged. “Suit yourself. It’s not glamorous.”
“I didn’t expect it to be.”
Sol’s yard concourses were never quiet.
The corridor outside the berth opened into a wide service spine crowded with cargo haulers, dockhands, traders, and crews on short leave. Overhead displays flickered with arrival schedules and berth rotations. The air smelled faintly of ozone, machinery oil, and a dozen different cuisines competing for attention.
They moved through it side by side, not touching, not speaking at first.
Mosby stopped at a fuel services kiosk wedged between a parts recycler and a stall selling fried noodles from a vat that had probably outlived several empires. He leaned on the counter, exchanging quiet words with a clerk who looked like he’d been born tired.
“Salt renewal slot in twelve hours,” Mosby said as they stepped away. “We’ll top off before we leave.”
Sael nodded. “Good.”
They found a food stall with actual vegetables — a luxury by spacer standards — and carried their trays to a narrow counter overlooking a wide viewport.
Beyond the glass, ships drifted in layered motion, tugs guiding freighters into cradles, shuttles flitting between platforms, traffic lights blinking in silent choreography.
For a while, they just ate.
Mosby watched the traffic. Sael watched him watching it.
“You don’t seem impressed,” she said at last.
“By Sol?” he asked.
“Yes.”
He shrugged. “Big is just big. Means more ways to get lost. More ways to get stepped on.”
Sael considered that. “Most people see opportunity.”
“Most people aren’t trying not to be noticed.”
She studied him a moment longer. “You’ve been doing this a long time.”
“Long enough.”
“Do you ever think you should have done more?” she asked quietly.
Mosby didn’t look at her. “Doing the job in front of you is already more than most manage.”
She let that sit between them.
Traffic flowed past in silent arcs of light.
Neither of them said what they were thinking.
But the distance between them had changed — just a little.
Back at the berth, the Talari moved through the Farther Star like careful ghosts, tools whispering, information flowing in quiet currents Mosby would never see.
They stayed at the counter longer than the food required.
Beyond the viewport, a bulk freighter rotated slowly under tug guidance, its hull scarred and layered with paint from a dozen ports. Smaller craft moved around it in careful arcs, every motion measured, anticipated, approved.
Sael rested her elbows lightly on the counter, fingers curled around the edge of her tray.
“You said most people aren’t trying not to be noticed,” she said.
Mosby took a slow sip from his cup. “That’s the trick.”
“Staying small?”
“Staying alive.”
She watched the freighter drift past. “You make it sound like those are the same thing.”
“Usually are.”
A pause.
“And when they’re not?” she asked.
Mosby’s gaze shifted, not to her, but to a patrol cutter sliding along a distant traffic lane, its transponder crisp and official.
“Then you pick the one you can live with afterward,” he said.
Sael was quiet for a moment.
“You’ve had to make that choice before.”
“Everyone has,” Mosby said.
“Not like you.”
That made him glance at her.
She didn’t look away.
“You disobeyed a direct order.”
Mosby’s thumb rubbed once, slow and unconscious, along the old scar over his eye before his hand stilled.
Mosby’s mouth twitched faintly. “That’s one way to put it.”
“You chose people over the mission.”
“I chose the people in front of me,” he said. “The mission was somewhere else.”
Sael absorbed that.
“In my line of work,” she said slowly, “we’re taught that the bigger picture matters more than any one piece of it.”
“Must make it easier,” Mosby said.
She looked down at her hands. “It’s supposed to.”
The noise of the concourse swelled and faded around them — footsteps, voices, the distant whine of docking clamps cycling.
“You don’t believe that,” he said.
Sael didn’t answer right away.
“I believe systems matter,” she said at last. “Structures. Stability. Without them, everything falls apart.”
“Yeah,” Mosby said. “But when something’s breaking right in front of you, you don’t get to tell it to wait for the system to catch up.”
Her lips curved slightly. Not quite a smile.
“You make it sound simple.”
“It isn’t,” he said. “It’s just immediate.”
Sael studied him openly now.
“You don’t seem to want anything,” she said.
Mosby snorted. “I want my ship to hold together and my food not to come out of a tube.”
“I mean beyond that.”
He looked back out at the traffic web.
“I had my turn at wanting big things,” he said. “Didn’t go great.”
“That doesn’t stop most people.”
“I’m not most people.”
“No,” Sael agreed quietly.
The words carried more weight than she intended.
For a moment neither of them spoke.
A transport liner drifted past the viewport, its hull lights tracing a slow, graceful curve toward an inner dock. Thousands of people aboard, each with somewhere to be, something they believed mattered.
Sael’s voice, when she spoke again, was softer.
“Do you ever get tired,” she asked, “of being the one who carries it?”
Mosby didn’t ask what it was.
“Yeah,” he said. “But dropping it doesn’t make it lighter.”
She held his gaze a second longer than she meant to.
Something shifted there — not dramatic, not declared. Just an awareness, newly sharpened.
Then she looked away first.
“We should get back,” she said. “Ted will have taken half the ship apart by now.”
“Only half?” Mosby said. “He’s slipping.”
She let out a quiet breath that might have been a laugh.
They stood, trays in hand, and rejoined the flow of the concourse — two figures among thousands, carrying more between them than either had planned to.
They didn’t speak much on the walk back.
The noise of the station wrapped around them again — cargo carts whining past, dock crews arguing in three languages at once, announcements chiming overhead with the patient indifference of infrastructure that outlived empires. The smell shifted every dozen meters: oil, ozone, spice, hot metal, recycled air breathed by a million strangers.
Sael moved through it smoothly, not hurried, not hesitant. But Mosby noticed she watched everything. Reflections in viewport glass. Security patrol patterns. The way people avoided certain corridors without looking like they were avoiding them.
Mosby watched her for another few steps, then said,
“That intelligence training?”
Sael didn’t look at him.
“Which part?”
“The way you check exits without turning your head.”
A beat passed.
“Logistics teaches you where people get trapped,” she said. “Intelligence teaches the rest.”
Mosby gave a quiet grunt.
“Yeah,” he said. “It does.”
They reached the service spine leading back toward their berth. Traffic thinned here, shifting from transient crowds to crews who knew exactly where they were going and had no interest in conversation. A maintenance drone hummed past at shoulder height, trailing fiber cable like a tail.
The Farther Star came into view around the curve of the drum — hull panels open, work lights glowing inside her like a ship laid bare on an operating table, mid-procedure but no longer bleeding.
Except there were fewer lights than before.
One of the Talari was sealing a panel. Another disconnected a diagnostic filament and coiled it with precise, economical movements. The third stood still for a moment, head tilted, eyes unfocused — receiving, considering, concluding.
Ted emerged from beneath the forward housing just as Mosby and Sael stepped onto the docking gantry.
“We are ahead of projection,” he said.
Mosby blinked. “That so?”
“Yes. Cooperative cognition improves efficiency.”
“Translation,” Mosby muttered, “you worked fast.”
“Yes.”
Sael looked over the hull. “How much fast?”
Ted glanced at a readout pad. “Structural grace restored. Thermal tolerances improved. I am now only mildly offended by the drive’s behavior.”
Mosby nodded toward the open bay. “She gonna hold together?”
“Yes,” Ted said. Then, after a fractional pause, “With personality.”
“Wouldn’t be my ship without it,” Mosby replied.
He checked the time on his wrist display. “Helion top-off window’s in six hours. We don’t miss that.”
“We will not,” Ted said. “I have already requested line access and pre-flush clearance.”
Mosby stopped mid-step. “You what?”
Ted tilted his head. “Efficiency.”
Sael hid a small smile.
Inside the ship, the air smelled like warm metal and solvent instead of strain and overheated composites. The Talari moved through the compartments one last time, hands brushing panels, verifying work without tools — like musicians tuning by ear.
One paused near Mosby as he watched.
Dark eyes met his.
A subtle nod.
Work complete.
No words exchanged. None needed.
Mosby looked around the cargo bay — crates secured, straps tight, nothing out of place. The Star felt… settled. Not fresh. Not new.
But ready.
He exhaled slowly.
“Good,” he said. “We refuel, we file departure, and we get small again.”
Sael glanced toward the station beyond the gantry, where traffic moved in endless layers.
“For how long?” she asked.
Mosby didn’t answer right away.
“Long enough,” he said at last.
Ted, halfway up a ladder, paused and looked back toward the hatch — not at Mosby, not at Sael, but toward the open corridor beyond the ship, where the station lights flickered against moving hulls.
“Attention,” he said quietly.
Both of them turned.
“Not on us,” Ted added after a moment. “Yet.”
The Star sat silent in her berth, systems resting, tanks soon to be full again.
Outside, Sol turned on — vast, indifferent, and never truly asleep.
Inside, they prepared to leave it behind.
Sael closed the billing pane with a controlled exhale.
The numbers did not linger. She did not let them.
Helion replenishment fees for the FTL alone eclipsed what most independent traders spent on fuel in a year. Line access surcharges. Purity certification. Containment flushing. The price of having pushed the drive hard enough to anger the salt.
Kryseum for the maneuvering thrusters barely registered by comparison — topped off as a matter of routine — though even that line item carried Sol’s quiet tax: volume pricing scaled to traffic priority, not need.
She understood, intellectually, why the galaxy ran on gates.
Direct FTL left scars — on ships, on accounts, on people.
Another set of confirmations scrolled in.
Cargo manifests.
Not bulk freight. Not contracts worth advertising. Small consignments pulled from different vendors, routed through maintenance depots and medical suppliers that didn’t bother asking where the ship was headed.
Emergency ration packs rated for long shelf life and short temp excursions.  Medical sealant cartridges. Auto-suture kits. Broad-spectrum injectables. Water recyclate filters small enough to fit in a locker, valuable enough to keep a crew alive when things went wrong.
None of it took much space.
All of it mattered.
Sael frowned slightly, scanning the list.
“This isn’t trade cargo,” she said.
“No,” Mosby replied from the cockpit. “It’s insurance.”
She looked up at him.
“You’re planning to be unreachable.”
“I’m planning to need what we’ve got,” he said. “Out there, the wrong shortage kills you faster than an enemy.”
Ted’s voice came softly over the internal channel. “Medical supplies are… prudent.”
Sael tagged the last crate for intake and authorized the transfer. Dockhands wouldn’t ask questions; small orders disappeared into ships like this every hour in Sol.
She filed the final confirmation and sent it on.
The deck vibrated beneath her feet as departure readiness propagated through the hull.
Departure felt routine. That was what made it believable.
Traffic control slotted them into outbound flow without hesitation. Their transponder became just another identifier in a sea of ships leaving humanity’s busiest crossroads.
Voices layered over the comms — clearances, advisories, queue acknowledgments — all crisp, professional, endless. Sol did not rush. Sol did not hesitate. Sol simply absorbed ships and released them again.
Mosby kept chatter minimal. His responses were short, correct, forgettable — exactly what a tired freighter captain sounded like. Ted stayed in engineering until the last possible moment, coaxing tolerances tighter than the ship had any right to maintain. Sael watched the traffic density map and did not look at the numbers again.
The Dawnreach gate filled the forward display — a luminous ring wrapped in construction scaffolds, fuel depots, and maintenance arms. Ships fed into it in steady arcs. Bulk cruisers dominated the inner lanes, their mass claims bending the queue geometry around them like gravity made visible.
A passenger liner slid into an adjacent lane.
Long, graceful. Bands of viewport lights traced its hull in soft gold, like a floating city at dusk. Sael recognized the class immediately — not the ship itself, but the design. She had traveled on one like it before. Wide promenades. Observation decks. Families who paid extra so their children could watch stars stretch and bend during transit.
She tracked it for a few seconds longer than necessary.
“Eight-point-one-five light-years,” Mosby said. “Short hop.”
Ted made a faint sound.
Not disagreement. Not quite concern.
Mosby didn’t ask.
Ted emerged into the cockpit just before sequencing lock, posture precise, movements economical. He stared at the lane geometry longer than necessary, head tilting by fractions as the flow resolved around them.
“Pattern density is correct,” he said slowly. “Timing is correct. But—”
“But what?” Sael asked.
Ted did not answer right away. His gaze drifted across the parallel lanes, the synchronized acceleration curves, the perfect obedience of motion.
“I do not like it,” he said at last. “The gate is restless.”
Mosby snorted softly. “This is Sol. They iron the wrinkles out before you ever see them.”
The liner ahead adjusted its position, thrusters whispering as it aligned perfectly within its corridor. Everything about it was smooth. Predictable. Safe.
Before either of them could respond, the sequencing tone chimed.
The Farther Star slid into the corridor.
Transit space formed around them — structured light, distant silhouettes of ships in parallel lanes, all motion guided and precise. The gate’s control systems took hold with firm confidence, mass and momentum folded neatly into place.
The vibration through the deck was faint, but it did not settle into a single rhythm.
Sael felt it then — not fear, not yet — just a mild, inexplicable tension. Like standing on a platform when a train passed too close, too fast.
For a moment, everything aligned too perfectly.
Ted’s claws tightened once on the edge of the console.
“Friend Mosby,” he said quietly, “if the flow shifts suddenly—”
“It won’t,” Mosby replied, eyes forward.
Ted did not look convinced.
The Farther Star held her line.
Guidance solutions updated and re-updated, each one accepted without correction. No flags. No cautions. The vibration through the deck smoothed, becoming something Mosby could feel without listening to — the quiet, confident hum of a system doing exactly what it was designed to do.
The kind of hum that let you relax by accident.
For hours, it was almost peaceful.
Then the exit chime sounded.
A gentle tone.
Routine.
Ahead, ships began making micro-adjustments as the corridor end neared. Tiny vector trims. Fractional attitude shifts. The normal, unconscious choreography of traffic preparing to unfold back into real space.
Mosby watched it with half his attention. This part always looked the same.
The cargo hauler in front of them drifted.
Just enough.
Not a maneuver. Not a course change. Just a lazy lateral slide, like a correction that came a fraction late — or maybe a fraction too much.
Mosby’s brow furrowed.
“Huh,” he murmured.
Sael leaned forward. “They’ll correct.”
The hauler’s guidance lights flickered as its system recalculated. New vectors fed in. Thrusters nudged.
One of the outer containers brushed the corridor boundary.
It didn’t scrape.
It caught.
For a heartbeat, nothing happened.
Then the front of the hauler slowed — not abruptly, not violently — as if it had struck invisible molasses.
The corridor field grabbed it, bleeding momentum away in an instant.
The rest of the ship did not agree.
The stern whipped down, striking the opposite side of the tunnel.
Ted inhaled sharply. “That is not acceptable.”
The stern swung wide, dragging a line of containers through the boundary field. Restraints failed one after another as stress cascaded faster than the systems could compensate.
Containers vanished in flashes of distorted light — not explosions, not debris — just absence.
“Lane deviation,” Ted snapped.
The hauler bent.
Then it broke.
Inertia kept the middle moving—snapping the ship in two and sending each piece cartwheeling. The forward section wrenched sideways, tumbling as the corridor chewed at its edges. The stern followed a heartbeat later, spinning the opposite direction, trailing a spray of cargo ripped free like seeds from a bursting pod.
Loose containers scattered across lanes.
One clipped a ship two corridors over.
Its field flared once.
Then it was gone.
Alarms detonated across the Farther Star’s cockpit.
More cargo tore loose. A long cylindrical tank spun end over end, tumbling wide.
It struck another vessel.
The discharge inside the corridor was blinding — a white bloom that warped the tunnel walls and rippled the structured light like heat through glass.
“What was that?!” Mosby shouted.
“Kryseum fuel tank!” Ted replied. “Ignition under gradient stress!”
The shockwave hit.
The Star bucked like a kicked animal. Mosby’s shoulder slammed into the restraints. Consoles flashed red. Somewhere aft, something heavy broke loose with a metallic shriek.
A panel burst beside Ted. Sparks sprayed. He hissed as molten fragments scored his arm. Blood followed, dark against the deck.
“I remain functional,” he said tightly.
The corridor filled with spinning metal.
Mosby was already moving.
He rolled the Star hard to port, nose dipping, sliding under a rotating slab of cargo that would have taken the cockpit off if he’d stayed level. The control yoke shuddered in his hands as he fed in lateral thrust.
“Just like dodging AA coming in hot,” he muttered.
Sael slammed a hand against the console as a container screamed past overhead.
“Are you out of your damn mind?!” she barked.
Another violent twist of the controls. The Star yawed sideways, engines howling as Mosby threaded them through a narrowing gap between a tumbling engine segment and a debris cloud thick enough to blot out the corridor walls.
“This isn’t anti-air!” she shouted. “This is a gods-cursed scrapyard in a gravity blender!”
Mosby cut thrust for half a second, letting a wave of debris outrun them — then punched it back in.
A container cartwheeled straight toward the cockpit, filling the forward display.
“Mosby—!”
He rolled knife-edge and dropped them half a lane. The container tore past close enough to rattle the hull like a struck bell.
“I swear to every saint on this ship’s hull,” Sael yelled, white-knuckled on the console, “if we live through this I am never taking a gate again!”
“Noted,” Mosby grunted.
Something detonated two lanes over. The shockwave slammed into them sideways. Sael was thrown hard into her restraints, breath punched out of her in a burst of profanity that escalated in both volume and creativity.
The Star shuddered, nearly slewing into a spinning hull plate the size of a shuttle. Mosby kicked the thrusters again, skimming past it with meters to spare. The deck screamed under the stress, metal complaining in sharp, familiar tones.
Ahead, the corridor twisted.
A passenger liner flickered into view — sideways.
For a heartbeat, Sael didn’t understand what she was seeing. Liners weren’t built to be sideways. They moved like cities, not debris.
Then the rows of windows resolved.
Bands of warm light streaked past the canopy. Observation decks. Promenade levels. She saw motion behind the glass — silhouettes tumbling, hands reaching, bodies thrown against transparent walls as gravity betrayed them.
Children.
The recognition landed fully formed, complete and merciless.
That class. That layout.
She had stood behind windows just like those. Watched stars stretch. Heard laughter echo through wide corridors.
“Mosby—” she started, but there was nothing to say.
The ship sheared cleanly in half.
Not an explosion. Not a flash.
A smooth, impossible separation, as if the universe itself had drawn a line and decided the rest no longer belonged together. The two halves peeled away from each other, internal lights flaring once — then tearing free into darkness.
Sael went silent.
Not screaming. Not frozen.
Silent in the way something goes quiet after it has shattered completely.
“Field collapse propagating!” Ted shouted. “We must exit now or not at all!”
Mosby didn’t answer. He was already forcing the Star toward a narrowing ribbon of stable geometry, riding the edge of coherence as the corridor walls shuddered around them. The structured light flickered, collapsing into raw gravitational noise.
The transit tunnel convulsed like a living thing.
Then it came apart.
The ordered field dissolved into chaos — collapsing vectors, gravitational shear, flashes of raw energy as ships were spat back into normal space without alignment or warning.
“Inertial dampers to maximum!”
“Already at limit!”
The Farther Star was thrown sideways, alarms howling as the universe lost patience with geometry.
Then — with a gut-wrenching lurch —
They slammed back into real space.

Silence didn’t follow.
Alarms did.
“Report!” Mosby shouted.
“Multiple structural stress warnings. Port maneuver cluster offline. Kryseum flow unstable,” Ted rattled off.
Sael grabbed the sensor panel. “Debris everywhere… I’m not seeing the gate. Mosby, the gate is gone.”
“What do you mean, gone?”
“It’s just—” She swallowed. “Wreckage. The ring’s missing a section. Like something took a bite out of it.”
Mosby didn’t answer.
He was fighting the controls.
The Star wasn’t flying.
It was spinning — a slow, unstoppable roll, mass imbalanced from the damaged thruster cluster.
“I can’t counter it,” he growled. “Thrusters are not responding.”
Ships burst from the broken gate at random angles — tumbling, venting atmosphere, trailing fire. Some drifted dark.
The gate itself flickered, a jagged section missing from its inner ring.
Emergency beacons began to scream across every band.
Sael stared at the display, hands shaking.
“How many…” she whispered.
Traffic icons blinked in and out. Some never came back.
“That liner…” she said. “There were people—”
“Yes,” Ted said softly.
Sael’s fingers were still on the panel.
She hadn’t realized they’d stopped moving.
The image of the liner replayed in her mind — rows of windows, warm light, motion behind the glass. Not abstractions. Not numbers.
Families.
Her breath hitched. The alarms blurred into a distant roar.
“Sael.”
She didn’t respond.
“Sael!” Mosby barked, sharp and loud enough to cut through the cockpit noise.
She flinched, eyes snapping to him.
“I need you here,” he said. “Right now.”
A beat.
She swallowed hard, forced air into her lungs, and her hands started moving again.
“I’m here,” she said, voice tight but steady. “Reading debris vectors. Updating collision envelopes.”
Mosby nodded once and turned back to the controls.
“We’re not dead,” he said. “That’s step one.”
Sael nodded, even as tears she hadn’t noticed blurred the display.
Behind them, the wreckage of a highway between stars slowly spread into the void.
And for the first time, it wasn’t abstract anymore.
Ted’s eyes flicked across the cascading red warnings. “External damage to the port reactionless array. We are venting Kryseum.”
Sael’s blood went cold. “We’re leaking fuel?”
“Yeah,” Mosby said grimly. “And if we don’t stop it, either it ignites… or we end up dead in space with no way to move.”
He was already reaching for the suit locker. “We’re going to have to go out and fix it. Or shut off the flow.”
Sael stared at him. “You’re going outside?”
“I will assist,” Ted said, pushing himself up from the jump seat.
He made it halfway to standing before his legs tremored. He caught the edge of the console, a sharp intake of breath escaping him before he could hide it.
The tear in his suit liner had soaked through. A dark, tacky patch spread across his left side, and the arm he braced himself with didn’t move quite right — the joint too loose, angles wrong.
Sael was at his side instantly. “Ted, you’re hurt.”
“I am… aware,” he said, trying to straighten. The motion made him wince despite himself.
Mosby turned, took one look, and swore under his breath. “You’re not going anywhere except the autodoc.”
“I can still function,” Ted protested, though his voice had lost some of its steadiness.
“You can supervise,” Mosby snapped. “From a padded drawer.”
Sael slid under Ted’s good arm, helping take his weight. “Come on. Sickbay. Now.”
“I object to the term drawer,” Ted muttered, but he didn’t resist as they guided him toward the ladder.
The ship gave another slow, nauseating roll. Sael tightened her grip, boots thudding against the deck as they half-walked, half-dragged him down the corridor.
Warning lights strobed along the walls. Somewhere aft, a system relay popped with a sharp crack.
They reached the medbay, and Mosby slapped the autodoc awake. The lid irised open with a soft mechanical sigh.
Ted looked at it, then back at Mosby. “I dislike this machine.”
“Yeah,” Mosby said, helping ease him inside. “It’s not thrilled about you either.”
Sael secured the restraints while Mosby pulled up the diagnostic interface.
“You can still see the external feeds from in there,” Mosby said. “Talk me through it.”
Ted hesitated only a fraction of a second.
“…Very well,” he said. “Friend Mosby, you will need assistance, though.”
The lid lowered, sealing him inside as the autodoc flooded with pale blue light.
Sael stepped back, heart hammering.
Minutes later, Ted was sealed in the autodoc, medical foam knitting over damaged tissue. The lid fogged briefly, then cleared.
Which left two of them.
Sael followed Mosby to the airlock and donned a pressure suit, diagnostics scrolling across her visor.
“I have not performed external repair under rotational drift,” she said.
Mosby checked her tether personally. “You’ve done worse.”
“I have not.”
“Then today’s your day.”
The outer hatch opened to silence — and stars in motion.
They stepped onto the hull, magnetic boots finding purchase that felt less comforting than it should have. The Star’s slow spin made every step feel like walking across the surface of a turning world.
In the distance, Dawnreach gate hung pale, distant, and shattered. Around them, wreckage glittered — the remains of ships that had been whole an hour ago.
“Eyes on the job,” Mosby said. “Not the scenery.”
They worked in careful, deliberate motions.
Clamp the damaged radiator vane. Cut away twisted conduit. Secure the temporary brace over the maneuvering cluster housing.
The Star rolled.
The horizon shifted.
Sael swallowed hard.
A section of warped plating came free as Mosby cut the final bolt.
“I’ve got it,” he said.
He didn’t.
The panel slipped, catching the edge of his thruster pack as the ship’s rotation carried it past.
The force yanked him off the hull.
“Caleb!”
He swung out on his tether, the Star’s spin turning him into a pendulum. His boots kicked at empty space as he rotated past the hull again — faster.
“Thruster’s misaligned!” he barked. “Can’t counter!”
Sael’s training lit up in her mind.
Secure the debris. Prevent further damage.
Instead, she pushed off the hull.
Her boots lost contact. She fired her thrusters in short, uneven bursts, angling into his path.
“Sael— don’t—”
She caught his arm as he swung past. The impact spun them both, tethers tangling.
“Brace!” she yelled.
She fired hard against their shared momentum. It was inefficient, clumsy — but enough.
Their lines snapped tight, dragging them back toward the hull.
They slammed into plating together, magnets catching with a bone-rattling thud.
For a long second, neither moved.
“You still in there?” Mosby asked.
“Yes,” she breathed.
They re-anchored in silence and finished the brace, movements slower now.
Inside, Ted was conscious in the autodoc when they returned.
“You survived,” he observed.
“Barely,” Mosby said.
They made their way back to the cockpit.
The comm panel was alive.
Emergency channels overlapped in a storm of voices.
“…multiple vessels unaccounted for—”
“…life support failure—”
“…requesting medical—”
Sael brought up the long-range view.
The Dawnreach gate hung at the edge of the display.
Dark.
The inner ring was missing a jagged section, construction arms frozen mid-deployment. No transit glow. No emergence flares.
No ships coming through.
“It’s gone,” she said softly.
Ted nodded. “Gate aperture collapsed. No further transit possible.”
Mosby leaned back, exhaustion finally finding him. “So the system’s cut off.”

“That’s what I do,” he said.
Korrin nodded, like that tracked.
They watched the storm for a while — waves moving in the dark, wind shifting, carrying the smell of rain and salt.
Then Korrin exhaled slowly.
“When Thalean Intelligence traced the Caretaker’s last location,” he said, voice quieter now, “they were going to send a retrieval team.”
Mosby didn’t turn his head.
“Yeah,” he said.
“I steered them toward you instead.”
That got a glance.
Korrin shrugged slightly, still watching the water.
“Sael was… ambitious,” he said. “Smart. Capable. But she believes in systems. In levers. I wasn’t sure what she’d do if she got there first.”
He paused.
“I’m not sure I would’ve done any better.”
Mosby took another drink.
“And me?”
Korrin’s mouth twitched, just barely.
“You,” he said, “have a history of doing the right thing for the wrong reasons. Or the wrong thing for the right ones.”
Korrin stood.
“You do the right thing,” he added. “Last time, I was wrong.”
He started back up the beach, swallowed quickly by the dark.
Mosby listened to the surf, to the low roll of distant thunder.
Then Korrin’s voice carried back to him, almost lost in the wind.
“But this time—”
A beat.
“—this time I was right.”
Mosby didn’t answer.
He just watched the storm come in.
The End.
